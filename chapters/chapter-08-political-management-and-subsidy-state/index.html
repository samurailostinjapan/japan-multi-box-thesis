    Chapter 8 — Political Management and the Subsidy State¶

    The pressures described in the preceding chapters do not enter politics as abstract questions of debt sustainability. They arrive as a higher electricity bill, a more expensive bag of rice, a mortgage reset, a hospital unable to match private-sector wages, a local railway seeking support, or a manufacturer asking for protection from an external shock. Governments respond to losses that are immediate, concentrated, and visible. The fiscal cost of that response is dispersed across future budgets, taxpayers, bondholders, and the currency.

    This asymmetry is the political core of Japan’s slow compression. The state can prevent a large share of an external shock from appearing immediately in household cash flow. It can reduce regulated prices, subsidize fuel distributors and utilities, transfer funds through municipalities, lower taxes, compensate selected firms, and finance reconstruction. These actions can preserve demand, restrain measured inflation, and prevent an unequal cost shock from becoming a broader social rupture. They are not economically fictitious merely because they are debt financed.

    The difficulty is cumulative. A measure introduced as a bridge becomes harder to remove when the condition it addressed—weak real income, energy import dependence, regional fragility, or inadequate wage growth—does not disappear. The next shock then arrives before the previous support has been fully withdrawn. Supplementary budgets become a second annual budget process. Contingency reserves become a financing channel for policies whose broad purpose is known even when their exact amount is not. Temporary tax reductions acquire an electoral constituency. One-off transfers establish a reference point for the next round of relief.

    The central question of this chapter is therefore:

    How does Japan’s political system convert immediate distributional pressure into recurring fiscal commitments, and why does that process increasingly collide with monetary normalization, market confidence, and the longer-term reforms required by demographic contraction?

    The answer is not that Japanese policymakers are unaware of the debt stock. Ministry of Finance documents state the constraint directly. Nor is it that all supplementary spending is wasteful. Disaster relief, energy-security measures, and support for households exposed to a sudden terms-of-trade shock can be legitimate uses of fiscal capacity. The narrower finding is that the distinction between emergency intervention and the normal fiscal stance has weakened. Political stability is purchased by transferring a rising share of current adjustment into the public balance sheet.

    8.1 Politics allocates losses before it allocates growth¶

    Macroeconomic policy is often described as a choice among growth, inflation, employment, and fiscal sustainability. In practice, the first political decision is distributive: who must recognize the loss now?

    An increase in the yen price of imported energy cannot be eliminated by decree. It can be assigned to households through higher retail prices, to firms through lower margins, to workers through weaker real wages, to utilities and fuel distributors through regulated compensation, or to the state through subsidies and forgone tax revenue. The same is true of higher interest costs. They can be assigned to borrowers, depositors, banks, the central government, or future taxpayers. Policy changes the incidence and timing of the loss; it does not abolish the underlying resource cost.

    Japan’s political incentives favor delayed and dispersed recognition. Households observe the retail price immediately. SMEs observe the input invoice and lending rate. Municipalities observe the service shortfall. Future debt service is less visible because it arrives gradually as securities are issued and refinanced. The government can therefore reduce present hardship by moving the obligation into a budget whose full cost will be realized after the immediate political decision.

    This transfer performs a stabilizing function. Without energy relief, a sharp import shock could force households to reduce consumption more severely and accelerate failures among price-sensitive firms. Without reconstruction support, disaster-affected municipalities would be unable to finance a national-scale response. Without transfers during an inflation shock, nominal wage gains concentrated among unionized and large-company workers could deepen inequality. Fiscal intervention can prevent a temporary loss from destroying otherwise viable balance sheets.

    The policy becomes compressive when three conditions persist:

    1. the shock recurs or the underlying exposure remains;
    2. the relief is not matched by a durable revenue source or expenditure reduction; and
    3. withdrawal becomes politically harder than introduction.

    Japan meets all three conditions in important areas. Energy import dependence outlasts each subsidy window. The breadth of real-wage gains remains uncertain. Regional service costs do not decline in proportion to population. Disaster risk is permanent even though each event is unpredictable. Debt-service repricing continues after a particular relief package expires. The political system is therefore managing repeated losses with instruments designed and presented as temporary.

    The term subsidy state in this chapter does not mean that Japan has replaced markets with direct state control. It refers to a narrower regime in which public transfers, tax preferences, administered-price relief, special funds, and supplementary appropriations are repeatedly used to keep the household-facing price of adjustment below its full economic cost. The resulting stability is real. So is the public liability.

    8.2 The supplementary budget has become a second fiscal baseline¶

    Japan’s Public Finance Act permits supplementary budgets when the state must meet an unavoidable obligation or when circumstances arising after the initial budget make expenditure particularly urgent. The mechanism is necessary. No annual budget can anticipate an earthquake, war-driven energy shock, pandemic, or abrupt change in the external environment with precision.

    The practice has expanded beyond that narrow function. The OECD’s 2026 survey observed that supplementary budgets have been used every year since 1947, that the annual expenditure ceilings governing initial budgets do not control the combined total, and that the absence of a concrete threshold makes the medium-term fiscal rule nonbinding. It recommended limiting supplementary budgets to large shocks and strengthening the formal conditions under which they may be used.1

    Recent budgets show why the issue cannot be reduced to one administration:

    Fiscal year Net supplementary budget Selected purposes Additional bond financing
    FY2023 ¥13.20T Price relief, wage and SME support, semiconductors, regional transformation ¥8.88T
    FY2024 ¥13.94T Household relief, electricity and gas, fuel oil, AI and semiconductors, Noto recovery ¥6.69T
    FY2025 ¥18.30T Price relief, child payments, local grants, strategic investment, defense, resilience ¥11.70T
    FY2026, first supplement ¥3.11T Energy-shock and international-situation reserves, local support ¥3.11T

    Sources: Ministry of Finance supplementary-budget overviews. Amounts are general-account totals after stated reductions in previously approved expenditure; figures are rounded.2

    The table does not establish that every item was unnecessary. It establishes that the initial budget no longer describes the fiscal year’s expected policy stance. By FY2025, the 115.2-trillion-yen initial general account had become approximately 133.5 trillion yen after the supplement. The supplement added 18.3 trillion yen in both revenue and expenditure, of which 11.7 trillion yen was financed by bonds.3 The difference is too large to treat as a minor technical amendment.

    The composition is equally revealing. The FY2025 supplement did not fund only unforeseen emergencies. Its 17.7 trillion yen of additional program spending included 8.9 trillion yen for everyday life and price measures, 6.4 trillion yen for strategic and growth-oriented investment, 1.7 trillion yen for defense and diplomacy, and 709.8 billion yen of additional contingency reserves. Individual items ranged from winter utility relief and local food support to research facilities, AI, shipbuilding, school infrastructure, healthcare digitization, and the 2026 Asian Games.4

    Many of these may be defensible policies. Their inclusion in a supplementary budget creates a planning problem. A research facility, industrial strategy, school renovation, or multi-year resilience program is not inherently unpredictable. When recurrent or long-duration programs are financed through late-year additions, the initial-budget ceiling understates the government’s revealed spending preference.

    The Board of Audit provides stronger evidence than the headline totals. It examined general-account supplementary budgets from FY2016 through FY2023 and found 185.5 trillion yen of additional appropriations. Public bonds supplied 148.7 trillion yen—74.5 percent of the financing. In the FY2022 sample, newly established line items funded wholly by supplementary appropriations had a 54.3 percent carry-over rate, compared with 11.1 percent for the entire appropriation. For 34 large projects, the full supplementary amount was carried into FY2023; almost half of the identifiable adjusted amount later became unused.5

    Carry-over is not proof of waste. Supply shortages, procurement delays, construction schedules, and program design can make multi-year execution rational. It is evidence against the claim that the full amount was required for immediate expenditure within the year of approval. If an appropriation is both urgent enough to bypass the ordinary budget process and too slow to execute, the distinction between emergency response and forward program funding has weakened.

    This produces a second fiscal baseline:

    Initial budget constrained by annual ceilings
    + late-year economic package
    + supplementary appropriations and reserves
    + tax measures and special-account spending
    = realized policy stance.

    Debt sustainability must be assessed against the final line, not the first.

    8.3 Fiscal presentation can shift without changing the liability¶

    Political management also operates through the language used to describe financing. Three statements that sound similar have different meanings:

    • no additional bond authorization;
    • no increase in total government debt;
    • no increase in the amount of bonds sold to the market during a specified period.

    The FY2026 first supplementary budget illustrates the distinction. Approved in June after the Middle East energy shock, it added 3.1135 trillion yen to the general account. The entire amount was financed by special deficit-financing bonds. The government simultaneously stated that it expected to reduce by approximately 3 trillion yen the FY2025 bonds scheduled for issuance during the April–June receipts-and-disbursements adjustment period. It could therefore say that projected market issuance in FY2026 would not rise even though the new supplement itself was debt financed.6

    This was not an accounting fraud. Stronger revenue or lower required issuance from the preceding year can legitimately create room within an issuance plan. The analytical error would be to convert “no increase in market issuance” into “no fiscal cost.” The supplement authorized a new claim on public resources. The offset prevented one gross flow from rising relative to the previous plan; it did not make the energy support free.

    The FY2025 supplement used a related presentation. Stronger-than-expected tax receipts supplied 2.879 trillion yen, non-tax revenues 1.016 trillion yen, and the previous-year surplus 2.713 trillion yen. Those resources reduced the bond requirement, but they were not permanent offsets for permanent policy. Revenue surprises can finance a one-year measure without improving the structural balance in the following year.7

    The same discipline applies to funds and reserves. A transfer from a government fund to the general account is revenue to the general account. It may also reduce a public-sector asset or a buffer earmarked for another purpose. Whether the consolidated fiscal position improves depends on the asset, liability, and future obligation—not the label attached to the transfer.

    This distinction becomes increasingly important as the government seeks to demonstrate simultaneous fiscal activism and market discipline. “Responsible, proactive public finances” depends on the claim that strategic expenditure will raise nominal GDP and future revenue enough to stabilize the debt ratio. That claim can be evaluated. It should not be strengthened mechanically by relabeling borrowing, using one-off revenue, or counting an asset transfer as if it created new national wealth.

    8.4 Energy relief is the clearest form of recurring temporariness¶

    Energy subsidies show how a justified emergency response can become a durable political instrument.

    Japan is unusually exposed to the external price of fuel. Its energy self-sufficiency rate was only 15.3 percent in FY2023, and fossil fuels still generated approximately 70 percent of electricity. A weaker yen and a disruption to Middle Eastern supply therefore enter household bills, transport, agriculture, manufacturing, and municipal services with unusual force.8 The government cannot remove that physical dependence through short-term monetary policy.

    Since the 2022 global energy shock, support has repeatedly been introduced, tapered, extended, stopped, and revived. The FY2023 supplement allocated 794.8 billion yen to electricity, gas, and fuel-oil measures, while noting that the amount reached approximately 3.9 trillion yen when previously approved resources were included. The FY2024 supplement added 1.032 trillion yen for fuel-oil price mitigation and 319.4 billion yen for winter electricity and gas support. The FY2025 supplement added 529.6 billion yen for winter utility bills and combined this with the abolition of provisional gasoline and diesel tax rates. The FY2026 first supplement then created 2.5 trillion yen of Middle East-related reserves and restored 513.5 billion yen already used for electricity and natural-gas relief.9

    The recurrence changes the relevant counterfactual. A subsidy with an end date is legally temporary. If the government repeatedly reinstates relief when prices rise, households and firms begin to treat the protected retail price as the political reference point. Removal is then experienced not as the expiration of aid but as a government-caused price increase.

    The policy has four immediate benefits:

    1. it raises real disposable income relative to the no-subsidy case;
    2. it limits the cash-flow shock to energy-intensive firms;
    3. it suppresses headline CPI and can reduce second-round wage and price pressure; and
    4. it buys time for supply adjustment, efficiency investment, or normalization in global prices.

    The same design creates four costs:

    1. broad price subsidies benefit high users as well as vulnerable households;
    2. they weaken the price signal for conservation and substitution;
    3. they expose the budget to the duration of a geopolitical shock; and
    4. they obscure the inflation rate that would prevail without fiscal intervention.

    Japan’s June 2026 CPI demonstrates the last point. Government gasoline and fuel measures reduced the energy contribution to annual inflation by an estimated 0.74 percentage point. The relief was real for consumers. The underlying import cost had been moved into the fiscal account.10

    The FY2026 sequence also demonstrates the funding risk. In April, the government argued that existing resources were sufficient and that no immediate extra budget was required. By early June, it had enacted a 3.1-trillion-yen supplement financed entirely with deficit bonds. By July 21, Kyodo reporting cited by Reuters indicated that several hundred billion yen more might be taken from reserves as gasoline-subsidy funding approached exhaustion, with the protected price threshold under review for September.11

    This is not evidence of deception. The duration and intensity of a war-driven shock were uncertain. It is evidence that the fiscal cost is an open function of time:

    \[ \text{Subsidy cost} \approx \text{unit support} \times \text{eligible volume} \times \text{duration} \]

    The government controls the first term imperfectly, the second partly, and the third hardly at all. A fixed appropriation can therefore become a recurring request without any formal change in policy.

    Energy relief also collides directly with the BOJ. Subsidies lower observed headline inflation and protect demand, while deficit financing can weaken confidence in fiscal restraint and support aggregate purchasing power. The BOJ must judge the inflation path after stripping out a policy whose duration is politically contingent. If the Bank tightens against underlying persistence, it raises the cost of financing the relief. If it delays because headline inflation is being suppressed, yen weakness can increase the subsidy required.

    8.5 Transfers and local grants convert a national shock into political discretion¶

    Direct transfers and local grants solve a different problem. A uniform price subsidy is administratively fast but poorly targeted. Income-tested support is more efficient in principle but requires current information about household resources and an apparatus capable of delivering benefits quickly. Japan has repeatedly combined the two.

    The FY2025 supplementary budget allocated 2 trillion yen to the Priority Support Local Allocation Grant, including a 400-billion-yen special addition for food-price measures. It also provided 367.7 billion yen for a child-rearing allowance responding to rising prices. Municipalities could use the grant for measures suited to local conditions, including food and shopping support.12

    Local discretion has genuine value. Heating exposure, transport dependence, industrial composition, and household structure differ between Hokkaido, metropolitan Tokyo, and rural Kyushu. A national formula cannot identify every vulnerable group. Municipal channels can deliver support through institutions already connected to residents and local firms.

    The tradeoff is fragmentation. Different municipalities select different instruments, eligibility rules, and vendors. Administrative speed can favor vouchers, rebates, and broad categories over precise income targeting. A central government transfer may therefore be targeted by geography or household type without being narrowly targeted by need.

    The proposed income-linked benefit system was intended to improve that architecture. By the June 2026 proposal, the government was considering approximately 600 billion yen in annual cash benefits for low- and middle-income households as a bridge toward a more integrated tax-and-benefit mechanism. The amount was designed to offset the remaining 1 percent food-tax burden for the target population.13

    This created a policy with two layers: a broad food-tax reduction for all purchasers and a targeted payment for selected households. The broad layer maximized visibility and administrative simplicity at the point of sale. The targeted layer attempted to restore progressivity. Together they also raised the static fiscal cost.

    Transfers become part of the subsidy state when they compensate for a structural gap that policy has not resolved. If broad real wages rise persistently, temporary household support can expire without a large loss of consumption. If essential prices continue to outrun the income of pensioners, nonregular workers, and low-margin service employees, each expiration recreates the original political demand.

    8.6 The food-tax decision crosses a fiscal threshold¶

    The proposed reduction of the consumption-tax rate on food from 8 percent to 1 percent was the most consequential political commitment at the evidence cutoff. It was not yet enacted by July 31, 2026. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi had instructed the ruling parties to prepare the measure, with final cabinet approval expected in August and legislation planned for the autumn Diet session. The distinction between announced intent and law must be preserved.14

    The proposal nevertheless changed the fiscal expectation set because it fulfilled a central February election pledge and followed months of cross-party negotiation. It would begin in April 2027, last two years, and mark the first reduction in Japan’s consumption-tax rate since the tax was introduced in 1989. The shift from a zero rate to 1 percent reflected implementation constraints, including the time required to modify point-of-sale systems. Politically, the accompanying targeted payment was intended to make the burden effectively zero for lower- and middle-income households.15

    The immediate argument for the cut is strong. Food inflation had remained above headline CPI, and food absorbs a larger share of low-income budgets. A reduction at the point of sale is visible, automatic, and difficult to miss. Unlike an application-based benefit, it reaches households without requiring them to prove eligibility.

    The distributional objection is equally strong. Higher-income households spend more yen on food in absolute terms and therefore receive a larger cash benefit from a uniform rate reduction, even if the benefit is smaller relative to income. A targeted transfer can deliver more relief per yen of fiscal cost. The IMF accordingly advised that support for households exposed to the cost-of-living shock should be temporary, targeted, and budget neutral, and argued against reducing the consumption tax.16

    The fiscal arithmetic is more damaging than the word “temporary” suggests. Daiwa Institute of Research estimated that the 1 percent rate would reduce annual revenue by approximately 4.4 trillion yen. Adding approximately 600 billion yen of targeted payments produces a static annual cost near 5 trillion yen. A broader estimate of the campaign’s original zero-rate proposal was also approximately 5 trillion yen.17

    Against the Cabinet Office’s projected FY2027 primary surplus of 1.4 trillion yen, the arithmetic is direct:

    \[ ¥1.4\text{T projected surplus} - ¥4.4\text{T tax loss} - ¥0.6\text{T transfers} \approx -¥3.6\text{T} \]

    This is a static estimate, not a forecast. It does not include positive effects on consumption, tax compliance, or nominal GDP. It also does not include offsetting spending cuts or alternative revenue that had not been specified at the cutoff. It demonstrates the scale of the offset required. Growth effects would need to be implausibly large to replace the direct revenue loss by themselves; the same Daiwa estimate placed the near-term GDP gain at only about 300 billion yen.18

    The financing promise therefore carries more weight than the tax decision. Takaichi stated that the government would not rely on debt issuance and would instead use non-tax revenue, proceeds from government funds and foreign reserves, spending reform, and other resources. A permanent annual tax loss cannot be sustainably financed by a one-time asset sale or a windfall surplus. It requires a recurring flow of revenue or a recurring reduction in another expenditure.

    The exit date creates a second credibility problem. Restoring the rate to 8 percent in 2029 would produce a conspicuous increase in food prices. The scheduled upper-house election in summer 2028 would occur before that restoration. Even if the legal sunset remains intact, parties would have an incentive to extend, modify, or replace the measure rather than accept responsibility for the increase. Temporary tax cuts are harder to reverse than temporary transfers because the higher rate is experienced at every transaction.

    The proposal therefore crosses a fiscal threshold. Previous packages compensated households while leaving the consumption-tax structure formally intact. The food measure would alter a central, broad-based revenue source linked politically to social-security finance. It converts relief from an expenditure decision into a claim on the future tax base.

    8.7 Disasters reveal why fiscal space has option value¶

    Natural-disaster spending belongs in this chapter for a different reason. It is the clearest case in which a supplementary budget is both legitimate and difficult to pre-fund precisely.

    The FY2024 supplement included 667.7 billion yen for recovery and reconstruction, including Noto Peninsula measures. The FY2025 supplement included 741.7 billion yen for recovery from Noto and other natural disasters and 1.55 trillion yen for national-resilience infrastructure. These appropriations combined immediate recovery with longer-term mitigation.19

    On July 28, 2026, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture. By the evidence cutoff, rescue operations and damage assessment were continuing. On July 31, the government announced that 61.6 billion yen of ordinary local-allocation-tax payments would be advanced to affected municipalities and promised additional budgetary support as needed. No defensible total reconstruction cost was yet available.20

    The appropriate inference is not that the earthquake “caused” a particular future supplementary budget. It is that fiscal space has option value. A highly indebted state with monetary sovereignty can still mobilize resources after a disaster. The question is the price and the tradeoff. Every recurring subsidy or unfunded tax reduction consumes part of the capacity that can otherwise be deployed when a genuinely unforeseeable event occurs.

    Disaster relief also demonstrates why a mechanical balanced-budget rule would be damaging. Reconstruction should not be forced entirely onto current residents of the affected region or financed only by cuts elsewhere in the same month. Borrowing can spread the cost across the population and over the life of rebuilt assets. The monograph’s argument is therefore not against deficit spending as such. It is against treating every politically urgent objective as equivalent to an unforeseen national emergency.

    8.8 Official forecasts are conditional on political restraint that may not survive¶

    The Cabinet Office’s July 30 medium-term exercise projected a 1.4-trillion-yen central and local primary surplus in FY2027. It also modeled more favorable long-run debt paths under strong productivity growth, rising labor participation, and large public investment. Like all official projections, the results were conditional on stated policies, economic assumptions, and the treatment of future measures.21

    On the same day, Takaichi announced her intention to proceed with the food-tax reduction. The juxtaposition exposed a recurring institutional problem. Bureaucratic projections describe the fiscal path under the policy set incorporated in the model. Political commitments change that set faster than the formal projection cycle.

    This does not make the Cabinet Office model misleading. A model cannot incorporate legislation that has not been specified or enacted. It means the published surplus is not a cash balance waiting to be spent. It is the residual of assumptions that must be re-estimated when policy changes.

    The same issue appears in international forecasts. The OECD’s June outlook projected fiscal tightening in 2027 in the absence of supplementary budgets. That qualification is not a minor footnote in a system that has adopted supplementary budgets every year. It is the central scenario condition.22

    Political commitments can invalidate a baseline through four channels:

    • a new recurring expenditure;
    • a temporary program that is extended;
    • a tax reduction without a permanent offset; or
    • an optimistic revenue assumption that is spent before it is realized.

    The government’s preferred response is that proactive spending raises nominal growth and tax revenue, allowing the debt-to-GDP ratio to fall. That mechanism is possible. Chapter 4 showed why it is insufficient by itself. Nominal growth produced partly through inflation raises revenue, but it also raises indexed spending, wages, construction costs, subsidy demand, and eventually interest expense. If the BOJ normalizes in response, the effective financing rate converges toward the marginal rate with a lag.

    Forecast discipline therefore requires a rolling policy ledger. The July 30 surplus should be shown as the official baseline. The food-tax package should be shown as a subsequent policy adjustment with an estimated static cost. Kumamoto recovery should remain an unquantified contingent obligation until official appropriations exist. A future autumn supplement should not be inserted into the baseline merely because historical practice makes one plausible. Separating those categories is more rigorous than either accepting the official path uncritically or assuming every prospective commitment is already debt.

    8.9 The Takaichi effect is acceleration, not origin¶

    Sanae Takaichi did not create Japan’s fiscal-dominance regime. Recurring supplementary budgets, energy relief, strategic industrial subsidies, low-rate debt finance, and delayed consolidation preceded her administration. The FY2023 and FY2024 packages already combined household compensation with long-duration investment and industrial policy.

    Her administration changed the speed and clarity of the political signal. The FY2025 supplement rose to 18.3 trillion yen, from 13.9 trillion yen in FY2024, and relied on 11.7 trillion yen of additional bonds. The February 2026 election then gave the LDP-led coalition a lower-house supermajority and weakened internal fiscal resistance. Takaichi restated the food-tax pledge as a mandate to end what she described as excessively tight fiscal policy and inadequate investment.23

    The coalition’s position sharpened the policy collision. Japan Innovation Party leader Hirofumi Yoshimura argued that the BOJ should retain authority to raise rates in response to the weak yen while the government used the budget to build an economy capable of absorbing the pain, including higher mortgage costs. He simultaneously supported rapid food-tax relief and consideration of foreign-reserve income as a funding source.24

    That combination is internally coherent as a short-run political strategy:

    permit monetary tightening to defend price stability and the currency
    + use fiscal policy to compensate the sectors hurt by tightening
    = preserve the coalition’s growth and relief agenda.

    It is fiscally expensive because the compensation weakens part of the restraint created by the rate increase and is itself financed in a higher-rate environment. The political solution to monetary pain becomes another input into the fiscal constraint.

    Markets need not believe that Takaichi intends fiscal disorder. They reprice the expected distribution of future issuance, inflation, tax revenue, and BOJ freedom. When the food-tax announcement on July 30 pushed the ten-year JGB yield up 5.5 basis points, investors were responding before legislation or new issuance existed. Political commitments alter prices when they change the probability-weighted future budget.25

    The Takaichi effect should therefore be defined narrowly:

    The administration accelerated price discovery by making expansionary commitments more explicit, weakening the expectation of near-term fiscal restraint, and exposing the conflict between household relief, strategic investment, BOJ normalization, and currency defense.

    This is an inference about transmission, not a claim that every rise in JGB yields or every decline in the yen was caused by the prime minister. Global yields, energy prices, U.S. policy, dealer positioning, and risk sentiment remained essential.

    8.10 Foreign reserves expose the asset-financing contradiction¶

    The Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account sits at the intersection of fiscal policy, the weak yen, and intervention.

    Japan acquires much of its foreign-reserve portfolio by issuing yen-denominated Foreign Exchange Fund financing bills. The account therefore contains foreign-currency assets and associated yen liabilities. When MOF sells foreign currency and buys yen to support the exchange rate, the yen proceeds are, in principle, used to redeem the financing bills recorded as liabilities.26

    The account also earns income. In FY2025 it recorded a surplus of 5.06 trillion yen, the second-highest on record. The weak yen raised the yen value of foreign income, while yields on assets such as U.S. Treasuries exceeded the cost of yen funding. Of the surplus, 3.13 trillion yen was transferred to the FY2026 general account, 1.34 trillion yen was retained in the foreign-exchange fund, and 585 billion yen was carried into the next year.27

    This creates three distinct concepts that political debate tends to merge:

    Concept Fiscal meaning Limitation
    Annual reserve-account surplus A flow that can partly support general revenue Varies with exchange rates, foreign yields, funding costs, and portfolio size
    Foreign-reserve stock An external asset and intervention buffer Financed partly by yen liabilities; selling it reduces the asset base
    Yen received in intervention Cash used principally to retire related financing bills Reduces gross assets and liabilities together; does not create equivalent net wealth

    The annual surplus is legitimate non-tax revenue. It is not a stable substitute for approximately 5 trillion yen of recurring food-tax revenue. A stronger yen, lower foreign interest rates, higher Japanese funding costs, or a smaller reserve portfolio can reduce the flow. Moreover, the 3.13-trillion-yen FY2026 transfer was already part of general-account revenue. It cannot finance a later policy a second time without another offset.

    The stock argument is more consequential. Takaichi adviser Takuji Aida argued in a June 24 submission to the Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy that yen obtained through intervention could redeem financing bills, reduce gross government debt, and create approximately 10 trillion yen of additional room for strategic investment. The submission explicitly stated that the latest intervention had expanded such room and criticized the simultaneous rejection of net-debt analysis and this gross-debt effect.28

    The accounting sequence is mechanically intelligible:

    Sell a foreign reserve asset
    → receive yen
    → redeem a yen financing bill
    → gross public assets and gross public liabilities both decline
    → net debt is approximately unchanged before transaction costs and valuation effects.

    If the government then issues new debt for domestic strategic spending, gross debt rises again and the foreign asset is not restored. The consolidated state has converted part of an external asset into domestic expenditure. That may be justified if the investment produces a high social and fiscal return. It is not the creation of free fiscal space.

    The contradiction becomes sharper when intervention and relief are combined:

    Weak yen
    → higher yen income and valuation in the reserve account
    → greater non-tax revenue available for household relief
    + higher imported food and energy costs that create the demand for relief.

    Defend the yen through dollar sales
    → reduce foreign assets and related liabilities
    → reduce the portfolio capable of generating future foreign income
    → claim lower gross debt creates room to borrow for domestic spending.

    The same weak yen that enlarges reserve-account income worsens the household problem. The intervention intended to reverse that weakness can reduce the income-producing asset base. Borrowing again after the intervention converts a currency-defense asset into fiscal capacity. Each transaction can be valid in isolation; together they demonstrate solution collision.

    This analysis does not prove that the July intervention was conducted to create fiscal room. Currency intervention is an MOF operation intended to address exchange-market conditions, and the official amount for July 30–31 remained unresolved at the cutoff. Aida’s proposal shows that an influential adviser supplied a fiscal interpretation of the balance-sheet effect. It does not establish the motive of the operation.

    Nor does it imply that Japan is close to exhausting its reserves. Official reserve assets remained approximately $1.287 trillion at the end of June 2026. The issue is not immediate adequacy. It is whether the same asset can be counted simultaneously as an intervention buffer, a source of recurring income, a candidate sovereign-investment pool, and a basis for new domestic borrowing without recognizing the tradeoffs among those functions.29

    8.11 Execution capacity is a fiscal constraint¶

    Political debate emphasizes how much money is authorized. Demographic compression makes the capacity to execute equally important.

    Large supplementary packages compete for construction labor, engineers, caregivers, municipal staff, procurement specialists, and project-management capacity. When appropriations rise faster than the economy’s ability to deliver the underlying service or asset, the result appears as higher prices, carry-over, or unused funds rather than proportionate real output.

    The Board of Audit’s findings are therefore more than a transparency problem. The unusually high carry-over of FY2022 supplementary items indicates that financial authorization and real implementation were not synchronized. This problem is likely to become more important as the workforce contracts and infrastructure needs rise.

    The FY2026 initial budget acknowledged part of the issue by moving some repeatedly supplementary measures into the initial budget and by increasing price- and wage-related allocations. That is an institutional improvement because it makes known obligations more visible before the fiscal year begins.30 It also confirms the underlying point: when a “temporary” program becomes predictable enough to normalize, its cost belongs in the baseline.

    Strategic investment faces the same test. A fund or bond authorization does not itself raise potential growth. The project must be selected, staffed, built, integrated into private production, and maintained. If labor and administrative capacity are binding, additional nominal spending can increase competition for scarce inputs before it increases productivity.

    8.12 Counterarguments and limits¶

    Eight counterarguments qualify the chapter’s conclusion.

    First, the government should smooth external shocks. Correct. A state with fiscal capacity should prevent a sudden energy or disaster shock from destroying viable households and firms. The argument is not for immediate pass-through of every loss. It is that broad support should have a stated objective, a transparent funding source, and an exit rule tied to observable conditions.

    Second, energy subsidies can lower inflation expectations. Correct. Suppressing a temporary price spike can prevent wage and price setting from incorporating an extreme shock. It can also allow the BOJ to avoid tightening against a supply disruption. The countervailing risk is that recurring relief supports demand, obscures the underlying price, and becomes harder to remove.

    Third, supplementary budgets are constitutionally scrutinized and approved by the Diet. Correct. They are not off-book expenditure. The problem is not absence of legislative authorization; it is that repeated late-year additions weaken the connection between the initial budget, expenditure ceilings, execution capacity, and medium-term fiscal projections.

    Fourth, strong tax revenue can finance temporary support without worsening the debt path. Correct for a genuinely temporary measure when the revenue surprise is not needed elsewhere. A one-year revenue windfall cannot finance a recurring tax reduction. The relevant test is the structural balance after inflation and temporary revenue effects.

    Fifth, cutting the food tax may strengthen consumption and growth. Correct. Lower food prices raise real disposable income. The effect weakens the chapter’s conclusion if it generates enough additional taxable activity to replace a substantial share of the lost revenue without increasing inflation or imports. Available estimates at the cutoff placed the near-term GDP effect far below the static revenue cost.

    Sixth, the food-tax rate can return to 8 percent after two years. Legally and administratively possible. The measure should be treated as temporary in the baseline unless extended. Political timing and the visibility of the restoration create extension risk; they do not prove permanence.

    Seventh, strategic public investment can produce assets and future revenue. Correct. Gross-debt analysis can overstate the burden of borrowing that creates a valuable public asset. Net-debt analysis and project returns deserve serious treatment. A foreign reserve sold and replaced with a productive domestic asset may improve welfare. The burden of proof is project-specific: the investment must generate a return exceeding its financing and opportunity cost, and the intervention buffer relinquished must be valued.

    Eighth, Takaichi’s mandate may permit reforms that weaker governments could not enact. Correct. A strong lower-house majority can support entitlement reform, tax redesign, energy investment, migration institutions, and consolidation of low-value spending. The thesis is weakened if the administration uses its mandate to finance temporary relief with durable offsets and to move recurring programs into a transparent medium-term framework. It is strengthened if the mandate is used principally to expand commitments while deferring their funding.

    Measurement imposes further limits. A supplementary appropriation is not identical to cash expenditure in the same year. A tax expenditure is not identical to a direct subsidy. Market issuance is not identical to net borrowing. A fund balance is not automatically available for general use. The reserve account’s annual surplus is not the market value of the reserve stock. A policy announcement is not enacted legislation. Political analysis must keep these categories separate precisely because public debate often does not.

    8.13 Finding: compensation preserves stability by consuming policy room¶

    Japan’s subsidy state is not evidence of imminent fiscal failure. It is evidence of a government that retains enough balance-sheet and administrative capacity to prevent a large share of adjustment from reaching households immediately.

    That capacity has protected social and economic continuity. Energy relief reduced bills and measured inflation. Local grants allowed differentiated support. Transfers protected households outside strong wage settlements. Supplementary budgets financed reconstruction, resilience, defense, and industrial investment. Foreign-reserve income supplied genuine non-tax revenue. None of those facts is compatible with a claim that the state has become powerless.

    The same record establishes the compression mechanism:

    Supplementary budgets have become large and recurrent.
    Emergency and long-duration spending are increasingly combined.
    Bond financing remains the dominant historical source of supplementary resources.
    Energy support is repeatedly withdrawn and restored.
    A proposed temporary food-tax cut would remove a major recurring revenue flow.
    One-time fund income and asset transactions are being discussed as offsets for recurring commitments.
    Execution lags show that nominal appropriations can outrun real capacity.

    Political management does not eliminate the underlying loss. It changes its location:

    Household bill
    → government subsidy
    → larger borrowing or lower public assets
    → higher future debt service, taxation, inflation exposure, or reduced capacity for the next shock.

    The Takaichi administration did not originate this structure. It accelerated it and made its contradictions easier for markets to price. The first FY2026 supplement, the food-tax commitment, energy-support extensions, reserve-account debate, BOJ normalization, and currency intervention all occurred within the same policy year. Each instrument remained operational. Their side effects increasingly entered the decision rule of the others.

    Chapter 9 integrates those interactions into the multi-box compression mechanism. The fiscal, monetary, foreign-exchange, household, corporate, demographic, energy, and political constraints are not parallel lists of problems. They form a system in which relief in one box transfers pressure to another. The subsidy state is the political transmission mechanism that keeps the system stable in the present while narrowing the range of future adjustment.
