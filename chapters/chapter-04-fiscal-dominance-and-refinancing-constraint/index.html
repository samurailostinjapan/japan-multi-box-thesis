    Chapter 4 — Fiscal Dominance and the Refinancing Constraint¶

    Japan’s public debt is usually discussed as a stock: a ratio above 200 percent of gross domestic product, a central bank holding an extraordinary share of the sovereign-bond market, and a government that has operated with persistent deficits for most of the past three decades. Those facts describe the scale of the inherited balance sheet. They do not, by themselves, identify the constraint that matters most over the next decade.

    The operative constraint is a flow. It is the annual cost of refinancing a large stock of low-coupon debt while the state continues to finance ordinary expenditure, demographic obligations, strategic investment, energy relief, disaster response, and political compensation. Japan does not have to refinance its entire debt stock at current yields in a single year. That is precisely why a sudden sovereign-funding crisis is the wrong baseline. The pressure arrives gradually, through the maturity schedule, and accumulates in the budget. Each year of higher marginal yields converts another portion of the low-rate inheritance into a higher permanent carrying cost.

    This distinction changes the fiscal argument. Japan is not “running out of money” in the conventional sense. It issues debt in its own currency, possesses deep domestic financial institutions, holds large public and external assets, and retains a central bank capable of stabilizing market functioning. The relevant question is narrower and harder: how much of the government’s annual revenue can be absorbed by debt service and aging-related expenditure before other policy becomes structurally dependent on additional borrowing?

    The answer cannot be read from the gross-debt ratio alone. It depends on five interacting variables: the primary balance, the effective interest rate on the outstanding debt, nominal growth, the maturity and holder structure of Japanese government bonds, and the political tendency to treat supplementary spending as an ordinary instrument of economic management. In the current regime, these variables are moving in the same direction. The average coupon remains low, but the marginal cost of financing has risen. Nominal growth has raised tax revenue, but it has also raised indexed expenditure and strengthened demands for relief from inflation. The debt ratio has declined, but interest costs are rising. The budget therefore looks healthier by one headline measure while becoming more exposed by another.

    That is the fiscal core of slow compression.

    4.1 The budget is larger than the initial budget¶

    The FY2026 initial general-account budget totaled 122.3 trillion yen. Tax revenue was budgeted at 83.7 trillion yen, other revenue at 9.0 trillion yen, and new government-bond issuance at 29.6 trillion yen. On the expenditure side, social security accounted for 39.1 trillion yen, local allocation tax grants for 20.9 trillion yen, and national debt service for 31.3 trillion yen.1

    These figures immediately establish two points. First, debt service and social security together absorb 70.3 trillion yen, equivalent to 84 percent of budgeted tax revenue. That comparison does not imply that particular taxes are legally earmarked to those two categories. It shows the scale of the government’s committed expenditure relative to its recurring tax base. After those two functions, only 13.4 trillion yen of budgeted tax revenue remains against more than 50 trillion yen of other general-account spending.

    Second, the initial budget is not a reliable measure of the state’s actual annual fiscal posture. In June 2026, only two months into the fiscal year, the Diet received a 3.11 trillion yen supplementary budget responding to energy-price and Middle East risks. The entire amount was financed through additional special deficit-financing bonds. It raised the FY2026 general account to approximately 125.42 trillion yen.2 The government argued that market issuance would not increase because roughly 3 trillion yen of previously scheduled FY2025 bond issuance could be cancelled after stronger revenue and unspent appropriations were taken into account. That claim was mechanically plausible at the time. It did not change the economic character of the supplement: new FY2026 expenditure was matched by new FY2026 deficit-financing authority, while a prior-year issuance requirement happened to fall.

    This form of intertemporal netting is important. It allows the government to say that a new package will not enlarge current market issuance even though the underlying fiscal commitment has increased. The debt market may be protected from an immediate supply shock, but the budget has still acquired new obligations. If the favorable prior-year adjustment is not repeated, the same policy in a later year requires additional issuance or a different offset.

    The FY2025 supplementary budget illustrates the scale that can emerge. Its general-account framework totaled 18.3 trillion yen, including 17.7 trillion yen in additional spending; when related tax reductions and special-account measures were included, the government described the national package as 21.3 trillion yen.3 These numbers must not be added interchangeably. The 21.3 trillion yen figure was the broad policy package, not a 21.3 trillion yen increase in the general account. The relevant point is institutional: a package of this size was enacted after an initial budget of 115.2 trillion yen, making the supplementary process part of the effective annual fiscal baseline rather than an exceptional correction.

    The distinction between the initial budget and the realized fiscal stance therefore needs to be formalized:

    Initial budget + supplementary appropriations + tax expenditures and special-account measures = effective annual policy stance

    Not every supplementary appropriation is wasteful, discretionary, or avoidable. Earthquakes, energy shocks, security contingencies, and recession can require rapid action. The problem is not the existence of a supplementary process. It is the migration of recurring policy into that process. Winter utility relief, gasoline support, SME assistance, wage subsidies, strategic-industry funds, and regional transfers repeatedly appear as urgent responses even when their underlying causes persist across fiscal years.

    Once that happens, the initial budget understates the state’s structural call on resources. A nominal primary surplus calculated before the next package can coexist with an expansionary realized stance after it.

    4.2 Debt service is not the same as interest expense¶

    Japan’s FY2026 national debt-service appropriation of 31.3 trillion yen is often misread as an interest bill. It is not. The total includes 13.0 trillion yen of interest payments and related expenses, with the remainder principally representing transfers for debt redemption. The distinction matters because redemption transfers are largely financed within a system that issues refunding bonds under the 60-year redemption rule. They represent a real budgetary and debt-management flow, but they are not equivalent to the economic cost of interest.4

    Three related measures should therefore be kept separate:

    Measure FY2026 reference What it captures
    Interest payments ¥13.0T Current carrying cost in the general account
    National debt service ¥31.3T Interest plus redemption-related budget transfers
    Gross JGB issuance ¥180.7T New financing, refunding, and other issuance channels

    The gross issuance figure is especially easy to misuse. The Ministry of Finance’s FY2026 plan called for 180.7 trillion yen of JGB issuance, of which 168.5 trillion yen was scheduled as market issuance on a calendar basis.5 Most of that amount did not represent an equivalent increase in net debt. Refunding bonds replace maturing obligations. Their issuance maintains the stock rather than financing 180.7 trillion yen of new expenditure.

    This does not make gross issuance irrelevant. It identifies a different risk. Net borrowing measures the expansion of the debt stock; gross issuance measures the volume that must clear through the market. A country can reduce net borrowing while still facing a large refinancing requirement. For Japan, that requirement means investors must continually accept the rollover of an enormous inherited stock, and the price at which they do so progressively enters future budgets.

    The Ministry of Finance estimated 1,145 trillion yen of outstanding government general bonds at the end of FY2026, or about 1,095 trillion yen excluding the authorized front-loading of refunding bonds.6 The stock had an average remaining maturity of roughly nine and a half years at the end of FY2025. Its weighted-average interest rate was still only 0.98 percent. That maturity structure is a buffer. It delays repricing. It does not prevent it.

    The maturity schedule shows the front of the rollover wave. On the FY2026 initial-budget basis, approximately 158.0 trillion yen of general bonds mature in FY2027, 109.5 trillion yen in FY2028, 76.3 trillion yen in FY2029, and 79.7 trillion yen in FY2030.7 These figures are not forecasts of net issuance, but they show the amount of old financing that reaches a pricing event. When replacement coupons exceed the coupons of maturing debt, the effective interest rate on the stock rises even if the policy rate later stops increasing.

    This is fiscal inheritance cannibalization. The low-coupon debt accumulated under zero and negative interest rates remains valuable, but each year of normalization consumes part of that inheritance. The deterioration appears gradual because the average rate moves far more slowly than current market yields. By the time the budget reflects the full effect, the higher coupons have already been locked into multiple vintages of bonds.

    4.3 The Ministry of Finance already models the acceleration¶

    The 2026 Debt Management Report provides a clearer basis for the debt-service path than broad claims about the debt ratio. Under the ministry’s 3 percent nominal-growth case, national debt service rises from 31.3 trillion yen in FY2026 to 34.7 trillion yen in FY2027, 38.0 trillion yen in FY2028, and 41.3 trillion yen in FY2029. Interest payments alone rise from 13.0 trillion yen to 21.6 trillion yen over the same period.8

    Under its lower 1.5 percent nominal-growth case, national debt service reaches 40.3 trillion yen in FY2029 and interest payments reach 20.7 trillion yen. The slightly lower debt-service number in the weak-growth case is not evidence that weak growth is fiscally safer. It reflects the way tax revenue, local grants, borrowing needs, and assumed interest rates interact inside the ministry’s mechanical budget projection. The overall general-account deficit is worse in the lower-growth case.

    The official sensitivity analysis is more revealing. If interest rates from FY2027 onward run one percentage point above the 3 percent-growth baseline, national debt service reaches 45.1 trillion yen in FY2029. At two percentage points above baseline, it reaches 48.9 trillion yen.9

    Fiscal year MOF 3% nominal-growth case Rates +1 percentage point Rates +2 percentage points
    FY2026 ¥31.3T ¥31.3T ¥31.3T
    FY2027 ¥34.7T ¥35.5T ¥36.3T
    FY2028 ¥38.0T ¥40.1T ¥42.2T
    FY2029 ¥41.3T ¥45.1T ¥48.9T

    This table resolves a central numerical question. A national debt-service cost near 50 trillion yen is not the Ministry of Finance’s baseline forecast. It is, however, almost exactly the ministry’s published FY2029 high-rate sensitivity. The stress estimate is therefore analytically legitimate when identified correctly:

    Observed: FY2026 national debt service is 31.3 trillion yen.
    Official baseline: MOF projects 40.3–41.3 trillion yen in FY2029, depending on nominal growth.
    Official sensitivity: MOF reaches 48.9 trillion yen in FY2029 if rates are two percentage points above its baseline path.
    Monograph stress path: approximately 50 trillion yen around FY2029–FY2030 if high marginal yields persist and political spending is not durably offset.

    The time lag matters. The FY2026 budget books an interest rate of 3 percent for the purpose of estimating debt-service costs, but that budgetary rate is not the same as the weighted-average coupon paid on the debt stock, the Bank of Japan’s policy rate, or the yield at any single maturity. It is an accounting assumption applied within the budget model. Market yields affect the realized cost as securities are issued and refinanced. A 10-year yield above the budget assumption does not immediately reprice the entire stock; it increases the cost of the bonds sold at that maturity and influences the broader curve.

    The slow transmission can create false reassurance. The annual budget remains financeable, auctions continue to clear, and the effective rate stays below market rates. Yet the direction of travel has already been fixed for the debt vintages issued at higher coupons. Fiscal pressure is not absent during the lag. It is stored.

    4.4 Why a falling debt ratio can coexist with a worsening fiscal position¶

    Japan’s recent debt-ratio improvement is real, but its composition matters. Nominal GDP expanded rapidly as prices and wages rose, tax collections benefited from inflation and corporate profits, and the effective interest rate on the outstanding debt remained anchored by bonds issued during the low-rate era. The denominator grew faster than the average carrying cost of the numerator. That combination can reduce debt relative to GDP even while the nominal debt stock and annual interest expense continue to rise.

    The standard debt-dynamics identity is useful:

    \[ \Delta d_t \approx \frac{r_t-g_t}{1+g_t}d_{t-1}-pb_t \]

    where \(d\) is the debt-to-GDP ratio, \(r\) the effective nominal interest rate, \(g\) nominal GDP growth, and \(pb\) the primary balance as a share of GDP. When nominal growth exceeds the effective interest rate, the “snowball effect” reduces the debt ratio. A primary deficit can then coexist with a falling ratio. When the relationship reverses, the same debt stock requires a primary surplus merely to prevent the ratio from rising.

    At a debt ratio of roughly 188 percent of GDP, a seemingly small change in \(r-g\) has a large fiscal effect. If the effective interest rate exceeds nominal growth by half a percentage point, the government needs a primary surplus near 0.94 percent of GDP simply to stabilize the ratio. At a one-percentage-point gap, the stabilizing surplus approaches 1.88 percent of GDP. Using FY2026 nominal GDP around 689 trillion yen, those amounts are approximately 6.5 trillion and 13.0 trillion yen respectively. The calculation is deliberately approximate, but it establishes the scale of the exposure.

    The Cabinet Office’s July 2026 projections make the same point through a more elaborate model. In its strongest growth-strategy case, the ratio of central and local government bonds to GDP falls from 187.6 percent in FY2026 to 170.6 percent in FY2040. In the second growth-strategy case it reaches 174.6 percent. In the current-trend case, however, the ratio bottoms near 180 percent around 2030 and then rises to 190.6 percent by FY2040. A lower-potential-growth sensitivity reaches 202.6 percent.10

    Those ratios use the Cabinet Office’s central-and-local public-bonds measure, not the broader IMF measure of general-government gross debt. They should not be spliced together. Their value lies in the direction of the modeled path. Even the government’s current-trend case no longer produces a permanent decline. The ratio turns upward when primary-balance improvement weakens and the effective interest rate catches up.

    The government’s favorable cases are also less reassuring than their ending debt ratios suggest. In the strongest growth case, the primary balance of central and local government reaches a surplus of 1.3 percent of GDP by FY2040, but net interest expense rises to 4.6 percent of GDP. The overall fiscal balance therefore remains in deficit and deteriorates to approximately 3.2 percent of GDP. In the second growth case, net interest expense reaches 4.4 percent of GDP and the overall deficit reaches roughly 3.4 percent.11

    This is the central contradiction in “growing out of debt.” Faster nominal growth improves revenue and the denominator, but the growth strategy also assumes rising wages, prices, investment, and market rates. Over time, those rates enter the debt stock. The successful-growth cases do not eliminate the interest burden; they produce larger primary surpluses capable of partially offsetting it.

    That distinction is frequently lost in political discussion. A falling debt ratio is treated as proof that fiscal space has been created. In the model, however, the ratio falls because several demanding assumptions hold simultaneously: real growth accelerates despite population decline, total factor productivity rises materially, labor participation improves, nominal growth remains firm, and primary surpluses persist even as aging and strategic expenditure continue. Failure in any one dimension does not cause an immediate crisis. It narrows the margin available in the others.

    The International Monetary Fund’s 2026 assessment is directionally similar but less optimistic about the later path. It projected interest payments to rise by roughly 0.3 percent of GDP per year as debt is refinanced, doubling from 1.5 percent of GDP in 2025 by 2031 and exceeding 4 percent by 2036. The IMF baseline allowed favorable growth-interest dynamics to reduce gross debt through 2034, followed by an increase from 2035 as interest and age-related spending accumulated.12 The timing differs from the Cabinet Office’s current-trend case, but the mechanism is the same: a temporary window in which nominal growth outruns the effective rate does not guarantee a durable solution.

    4.5 Supplementary budgets convert shocks into structure¶

    The refinancing problem would be easier to manage if the primary budget adjusted automatically as interest costs rose. Japanese politics works in the opposite direction. The same inflation that raises nominal revenue reduces household purchasing power, increases procurement costs, raises social-security outlays, and creates pressure for tax relief and subsidies. Fiscal improvement on the revenue side generates demands for fiscal expansion on the expenditure side.

    The June 2026 supplementary budget is a compact example. The government restored 513.5 billion yen to general contingency reserves after approving summer electricity and gas assistance, then created a separate 2.5 trillion yen reserve for Middle East and energy-related contingencies. The appropriation was not payment for energy already consumed. It was fiscal capacity reserved for a continuing shock.13 This was rational risk management in isolation. It also demonstrated that the initial budget did not contain enough durable room to absorb the policy consequences of energy dependence and a weak currency.

    The deeper issue is recurrence. A subsidy that bridges a short, exogenous price spike can expire. A subsidy that compensates households for a structurally weak yen, recurring winter demand, and persistent import exposure becomes part of the political baseline. Withdrawal then produces a visible price increase even if the underlying wholesale price is unchanged. The state has not removed inflation; it has changed the payer and controlled the timing of recognition.

    The consumption-tax proposal announced in July 2026 would intensify the same pattern. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said the government intended to lower the tax rate on food from 8 percent to 1 percent for two years beginning in April 2027 and would seek non-debt financing through expenditure reform and non-tax revenue. At the July 31 evidence cutoff, the measure had been announced but not enacted, and its permanent financing had not been specified in legislation. Contemporary estimates placed the direct annual revenue loss near 4.4–5.0 trillion yen before related transfers.14

    It would be analytically wrong to insert that full amount into the current budget as if the law were already in force. It would be equally wrong to leave it out of the forward fiscal risk. The July Cabinet Office model projected a 1.4 trillion yen central-and-local primary surplus for FY2027 after assuming 10 trillion yen of additional annual fiscal expenditure.15 A food-tax reduction of the reported size, without a durable offset, is several times larger than that projected surplus. The proposal therefore tests whether the official primary-balance path is a policy commitment or merely the residual of assumptions made before the next relief measure.

    The proposed use of non-tax revenue does not settle the matter. One-off asset proceeds, fund surpluses, and foreign-exchange-account income can finance a temporary measure in a cash sense. They do not create a recurring tax base. If an annual tax reduction is financed by drawing down an asset or transferring an exceptional surplus, the first year may avoid additional bond issuance while the state’s balance sheet weakens. When the temporary resource is exhausted, the tax reduction must expire, another asset must be sold, expenditure must be cut, or borrowing must rise.

    The temporary label is itself politically unstable. Restoring a consumption tax from 1 percent to 8 percent would create an abrupt and highly visible increase in food prices. The government may intend to restore the rate after two years, but households and firms will make decisions around the lower rate, and the restoration would approach the 2028 political cycle. The fiscal risk is not that the measure is certain to become permanent. It is that temporary relief creates a new constituency and a new reference price, raising the political cost of expiration.

    This is how supplementary budgets and temporary tax measures narrow policy freedom without producing a conventional funding crisis. Each measure can be financed. The cumulative structure becomes harder to reverse.

    4.6 Gross debt, net debt, and the asset argument¶

    Japan’s large public asset position is a genuine counterweight to gross debt. The Ministry of Finance’s 2026 report placed general-government gross debt near 1,409 trillion yen and net debt near 537 trillion yen after subtracting roughly 872 trillion yen of financial assets, although the gross and net figures refer to defined statistical boundaries and market valuations.16 Any serious debt analysis must acknowledge this balance sheet.

    The inference often drawn from it is too broad. Government assets reduce net indebtedness and generate income, but they are not fully interchangeable with tax revenue or liquid debt-service capacity. The portfolio includes assets with different owners, mandates, liquidity, currency exposure, and political constraints. Pension assets support pension obligations. Fiscal-loan assets are matched against financing functions. Foreign reserves support currency and external-liquidity policy. Equity stakes, infrastructure, and loans cannot all be liquidated rapidly without economic or political consequences.

    An asset sale can reduce gross debt if the proceeds retire liabilities. It does not automatically improve net worth: an asset and a liability disappear together. If the same debt capacity is then used to finance current expenditure, the transaction has converted a financial asset into spending. That may be justified, especially if the spending creates a productive asset with a high return. It should not be described as free fiscal space.

    Foreign-exchange reserves make the point sharply. Selling dollar assets to buy yen can reduce the reserve portfolio and, depending on the financing structure, allow associated financing bills to be retired. Gross public debt may decline. Net public debt changes little at the moment of exchange because both the foreign asset and the yen liability fall. If the government subsequently borrows to finance domestic spending, it has effectively exchanged part of its external asset buffer for a domestic fiscal program.

    The relevant test is therefore not whether Japan owns assets. It is whether the income, liquidity, and institutional availability of those assets match the expenditure being justified. Recurring tax relief financed by nonrecurring asset conversion fails that test unless the policy also creates a durable offset.

    4.7 Domestic funding remains powerful, but marginal pricing is changing¶

    The claim that Japan’s debt is domestically funded remains partly true. It is no longer a complete description of the JGB market.

    At the end of March 2026, the Bank of Japan held 42.2 percent of government bonds and treasury bills on a market-value basis. Depository institutions held 13.6 percent; insurers and pension funds, 16.4 percent; public pensions, 6.4 percent; households, 1.7 percent; and overseas investors, 13.7 percent.17 On the narrower measure excluding some categories and separating central-government securities, the BOJ share was higher. The exact percentage depends on the instrument boundary. The structural conclusion does not: the central bank remains the dominant holder, while private domestic institutions still own a large share.

    Those holdings provide a formidable stability mechanism. Domestic banks need high-quality liquid assets. Insurers match long-duration liabilities. Pension funds maintain strategic allocations. The BOJ can alter purchase operations when market functioning deteriorates. These investors do not behave like a single leveraged foreign fund capable of abandoning the market in one trade.

    The buffer nevertheless has limits. The BOJ is reducing purchases and allowing its holdings to decline as bonds mature. A BOJ board member stated in May 2026 that the Bank held about half of outstanding JGB issuance, that annual purchases had once exceeded 130 trillion yen, and that purchases were scheduled to fall below 30 trillion yen in FY2027 against annual government-security issuance near 180 trillion yen.18 The transition requires other buyers to absorb a larger marginal flow at yields they consider adequate.

    Foreign investors are more important to that price-discovery process than their ownership share implies. At the end of December 2025 they held 12.8 percent of JGBs including treasury bills, but accounted for 47.2 percent of secondary-market spot transactions and 72.7 percent of futures trading.19 Their influence is concentrated in liquidity, hedging, relative-value trades, and the marginal price rather than long-term ownership alone.

    This does not mean foreigners “control” the JGB market or that domestic absorption has disappeared. It means an auction can succeed at a materially higher yield and thereby validate a new funding cost. The government receives the money; the fiscal constraint still tightens. A high bid-to-cover ratio cannot erase that result if buyers demanded a larger concession.

    The holder structure therefore supports the slow-compression thesis more than a crisis thesis. Japan retains enough institutional capacity to avoid disorder under most conditions. That same capacity permits higher costs to enter gradually, reducing the likelihood of a dramatic break while increasing the likelihood of prolonged budgetary adjustment.

    4.8 Counterarguments and limits¶

    Four counterarguments deserve direct treatment.

    First, the Bank of Japan can suppress yields. It can. Renewed large-scale purchases, fixed-rate operations, or an explicit yield cap could interrupt a disorderly selloff. The cost is not technical inability but policy collision. Suppression while inflation remains above target can weaken the yen, increase imported inflation, enlarge the BOJ’s market footprint, and make the eventual exit more difficult. Yield control shifts the pressure; it does not remove it.

    Second, inflation can reduce the debt burden. It can reduce the ratio while the effective rate remains below nominal growth. The benefit is strongest during the transition, when revenue and nominal GDP reprice faster than the inherited debt stock. It weakens as new coupons enter the stock and as spending responds to wages, prices, and household relief. Inflation is not fiscally useless. It is politically and temporally conditional.

    Third, Japan has a current-account surplus and large net foreign assets. Those assets strengthen external solvency and reduce the risk of a classic foreign-currency crisis. They do not place the private sector’s foreign assets at the unrestricted disposal of the general account. Japan can remain externally wealthy while the government’s annual budget becomes more constrained.

    Fourth, growth-oriented investment could raise productivity enough to stabilize the system. This is the strongest constructive counterargument. Productive public investment can raise potential growth, private capital formation, and tax revenue. The problem is not the possibility of a positive return. It is the burden of proof created by scale and timing. The return must be large enough, broad enough, and fast enough to exceed demographic drag, the cost of financing, and the tendency for temporary support to become structural. The Cabinet Office’s own scenario range shows how sensitive the debt path is to those assumptions.

    These counterarguments weaken any prediction of imminent insolvency. They do not restore the policy freedom of the zero-rate era.

    4.9 Finding: the constraint is fiscal oxygen¶

    Japan’s fiscal position is best understood neither as an approaching bankruptcy nor as an unlimited sovereign-currency balance sheet. It is a system consuming inherited fiscal oxygen.

    The inheritance consists of low coupons, long maturities, domestic institutional demand, BOJ holdings, public assets, and a period in which nominal growth exceeds the effective interest rate. These buffers remain large. They are also being used simultaneously. Higher marginal yields consume the low-coupon inheritance; repeated supplementary budgets consume revenue windfalls; tax relief consumes future tax capacity; reserve and asset arguments consume balance-sheet flexibility; and aging consumes an increasing share of primary expenditure.

    The result is not a single fiscal cliff. It is a narrowing corridor:

    higher yields
    → gradual refinancing at higher coupons
    → rising interest and debt-service flows
    → less room for ordinary policy
    → greater reliance on supplementary borrowing and one-off revenue
    → stronger market scrutiny of the next package
    → greater pressure on the Bank of Japan to contain yields.

    That final step establishes fiscal dominance in the operational sense used throughout this monograph. The Bank of Japan remains legally independent and can still raise rates. The fiscal scale of its decisions nevertheless constrains the range over which monetary policy can move without activating debt-service, credit, banking, and political pressure.

    Chapter 5 examines the other side of that constraint. The BOJ can defend inflation credibility and the currency by tightening, but tightening accelerates the refinancing mechanism described here. If it does not tighten, the yen becomes the pressure valve, importing the inflation that generates further fiscal relief. The monetary and fiscal boxes are therefore not parallel problems. They are one feedback system.
