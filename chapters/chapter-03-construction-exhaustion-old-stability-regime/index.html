    Chapter 3 — The Construction and Exhaustion of the Old Stability Regime¶

    Japan’s post-bubble stability was not an illusion. It was an institutional achievement built from policies and balance sheets that prevented a damaged banking system, a deflationary economy, and a rapidly expanding public debt from producing disorder at the same time. The state replaced private demand when firms and households repaired their finances. Banks were recapitalized and nonperforming loans were gradually resolved. The Bank of Japan drove short- and long-term rates toward zero, supplied abundant liquidity, and eventually removed a large share of government-bond duration from the market. Domestic institutions recycled private financial surpluses into public liabilities. Corporate caution weakened growth but strengthened balance sheets. Japan’s external assets generated income and reduced dependence on foreign-currency finance.

    The same structure imposed costs. Adjustment was slow. Weak firms and low-return assets remained in place. Wage and price growth became inert. Bank profitability was compressed. The JGB market lost depth as the Bank of Japan’s holdings increased. Fiscal policy expanded without creating a durable primary surplus. Household savers received little return on safe assets. These were not side effects outside the regime. They were part of the price paid for continuity.

    For most of the period, the trade was sustainable because the costs reinforced the stabilizers. Corporate caution reduced borrowing demand and inflation. Low inflation allowed low nominal rates. Low rates reduced debt service. A large domestic saving-investment surplus supplied funding. A strong external asset position insulated the currency from the external rollover risks faced by high-debt emerging markets. Political incrementalism distributed losses over time rather than forcing one sector to absorb them at once.

    The regime is now weakening because its components no longer point in the same direction. The behavior that suppressed wages and prices has begun to change. The Bank of Japan has left negative rates and yield-curve control. Marginal government borrowing costs are rising before the public balance sheet has been repaired. Demographic contraction is moving from a disinflationary influence into a labor- and service-capacity constraint. The yen and imported energy prices are transmitting external pressure into households and the budget. Political compensation is increasing when monetary normalization requires greater fiscal discipline.

    The old regime did not fail on a single date. It is being consumed.

    3.1 From balance-sheet crisis to managed adjustment¶

    The first half of the 1990s destroyed the assumptions on which the late-bubble financial system had operated. Equity and land prices fell, collateral values deteriorated, and the banking system accumulated nonperforming loans. Firms entered a prolonged period of deleveraging and balance-sheet repair. The Asian financial crisis and Japan’s domestic banking failures in 1997 intensified caution. By the late 1990s, deflation was not simply a fall in the consumer-price index; it was the nominal expression of weak demand, impaired intermediation, debt reduction, and defensive corporate behavior.

    The policy response was gradual. Authorities protected deposits and liquidity, recapitalized banks, expanded public credit, used fiscal stimulus, and eventually forced greater recognition of bad assets. This sequencing reduced the probability of a disorderly liquidation but prolonged the adjustment. Research on Japan’s post-crisis experience identifies delayed bank recapitalization and incomplete restructuring as major reasons the recovery remained weak.1 The delay was economically costly. It was also institutionally stabilizing in the narrow sense that credit losses were spread across time, the banking system remained intact, and mass foreclosure was avoided.

    The resulting economy was not frozen. Export industries remained competitive, households retained large financial assets, corporations reduced leverage, and public infrastructure and social services continued to function. But the private sector’s objective changed. Firms that had experienced the destruction of collateral and the danger of debt prioritized liquidity, retained earnings, and financial independence. Banks facing weak borrowers and later compressed margins prioritized balance-sheet survival. Households facing uncertain employment and aging increased precautionary behavior.

    The Bank of Japan’s retrospective review attributes part of the decline in Japan’s natural rate of interest to the cautious risk-taking behavior of firms after the bubble, lower potential growth, and demographic change. It also describes the eventual entrenchment of a norm in which wages and prices were assumed not to rise easily.2 That norm was not merely psychological. It was the repeated outcome of firms protecting volumes and employment by suppressing price increases, workers accepting limited nominal wage growth in exchange for employment stability, and policymakers supporting aggregate demand without forcing rapid resource reallocation.

    The old stability regime therefore began as a response to private balance-sheet damage. It became a durable political economy after the immediate crisis had passed.

    3.2 Deflation became a fiscal asset¶

    Deflation is usually described as a burden on debtors because it raises the real value of nominal liabilities. That was true for Japanese firms and households during the early post-bubble adjustment. For the sovereign balance sheet, however, the subsequent low-inflation regime created a different effect. It allowed the government to issue and refinance debt at extraordinarily low nominal rates for a prolonged period.

    Japan’s fiscal deficits did not disappear. Their carrying cost was suppressed. As short rates approached zero and the yield curve flattened, successive vintages of JGBs entered the debt stock at progressively lower coupons. The Ministry of Finance also lengthened the maturity structure. By the end of FY2025, the average remaining maturity of outstanding government bonds was roughly nine and a half years and the weighted-average interest rate was approximately 0.98 percent.3

    That combination turned time into the government’s principal buffer. A long maturity schedule prevented market-rate changes from repricing the entire stock. Low coupons reduced the interest component of the annual budget even as gross debt increased. Each year of near-zero yields extended the period before the fiscal cost of the accumulated stock became visible.

    The mechanism can be stated directly:

    persistent low inflation
    → low policy and market rates
    → low coupons on new and refinanced JGBs
    → slow effective-rate transmission through long maturities
    → fiscal capacity to run deficits without an immediate interest shock.

    This was not costless monetary financing. The Ministry of Finance still issued debt and investors still held claims on the state. Nor was it proof that the debt stock could grow without limit. It was a powerful intertemporal subsidy: the economic consequences of borrowing were deferred because the nominal price of time was exceptionally low.

    The fiscal benefit helps explain why gross-debt warnings produced little immediate market response. Investors were not pricing the debt ratio in isolation. They were pricing a yen liability within a disinflationary economy, a domestic institutional system, a long maturity structure, and an increasingly active central bank. Under those conditions, the annual flow remained manageable even when the stock appeared extreme.

    3.3 Domestic intermediation closed the funding circuit¶

    The old JGB market was sustained by more than household patriotism or generic “home bias.” Japan’s financial architecture converted domestic savings into demand for government liabilities.

    Households held deposits, insurance products, pension claims, and postal savings rather than building JGB portfolios directly. Banks, insurers, pension funds, and public financial institutions then allocated part of those funds into government securities. Banks required liquid, low-risk yen assets. Life insurers and pension funds required duration to match long-dated liabilities. The postal system and public institutions operated under mandates and portfolio structures that favored domestic fixed income. Regulatory and collateral frameworks increased the utility of JGBs throughout the system.

    Hoshi and Ito described this closed funding circuit as a central explanation for the persistence of low JGB yields. Their warning was that an aging population and declining private saving could eventually weaken the capacity of domestic investors to absorb a growing public balance sheet.4 The timing of that adjustment proved slower than a simple household-saving model implied because the institutional investor base, corporate financial surpluses, and Bank of Japan purchases grew in importance.

    Domestic ownership reduced run risk. A bank or insurer managing yen liabilities does not evaluate JGBs like a foreign leveraged fund evaluating an unhedged sovereign position. It has structural reasons to hold safe domestic assets and can accept lower yields than an investor demanding currency and political-risk compensation. A broad domestic base also allows the government to alter maturity, issuance composition, and distribution channels without depending on one external buyer class.

    This did not eliminate price risk. It changed the form of adjustment. Domestic institutions could absorb more debt at low yields, but the cost appeared through low investment returns, compressed margins, duration exposure, and reduced portfolio flexibility. The state was less exposed to an abrupt foreign funding stop because households and institutions absorbed a continuing financial claim on future domestic income.

    The funding circuit was therefore stabilizing at the level of the sovereign and distributive at the level of the economy. It placed part of the fiscal burden inside deposits, insurance returns, pension portfolios, and bank balance sheets rather than on an explicit annual tax bill.

    3.4 The Bank of Japan became the market’s architect¶

    The Bank of Japan’s role evolved in stages. It adopted the zero-interest-rate policy in 1999, quantitative easing in March 2001, comprehensive monetary easing in 2010, quantitative and qualitative monetary easing in April 2013, a negative policy rate in January 2016, and QQE with yield-curve control in September 2016.5

    Each stage expanded the Bank’s influence over financial conditions. Early quantitative easing targeted the quantity of current-account balances. QQE greatly increased the scale and maturity of JGB purchases. Negative rates pushed the lower bound below zero. Yield-curve control moved from influencing the curve through purchases to publicly targeting the approximate level of the 10-year yield, backed by fixed-rate purchase operations and a large balance sheet.

    The monetary objective was to escape deflation and achieve the 2 percent price-stability target. The fiscal effect was inseparable from the implementation. Large-scale purchases lowered the term premium, compressed yields across maturities, and removed duration from private portfolios. The government could issue debt into a market in which the central bank was continuously altering the supply-demand balance.

    The Bank’s 2024 review concluded that unconventional easing had positive effects on economic activity and prices, although the effect on prices was smaller than originally expected because inflation expectations and wage-price behavior were difficult to change. It also found negative effects on JGB-market functioning and a contraction in bank lending margins, while noting no clear evidence at that point that financial intermediation had been impaired in aggregate.6

    That balanced assessment is important. The BOJ did not merely “print money” to fund the state, and the program cannot be reduced to a failed inflation experiment. It lowered real and nominal financing costs, supported asset prices and credit, reduced the probability of deflationary contraction, and gave fiscal policy time. At the same time, its growing stock of JGBs reduced the amount available for private trading and altered yield formation.

    Bank of Japan research on individual JGB issues finds a nonlinear relationship. Purchase flows can increase transaction volume by facilitating trades, but a large BOJ ownership share reduces circulating supply, widens bid-ask spreads after thresholds are crossed, and distorts the yield curve. Continuous fixed-rate operations reinforce that effect.7

    The BOJ’s success therefore changed the nature of the market it was stabilizing. It prevented yields from becoming the clearing price of fiscal risk, but private price discovery weakened as a result. Once yield-curve control ended, the state did not return to the pre-QQE market. It entered normalization with the central bank still holding an enormous stock and private investors being asked to rebuild a marginal pricing role.

    3.5 Corporate caution financed public continuity¶

    The corporate sector’s post-bubble transformation was another stabilizer. Japanese firms moved from being major net borrowers toward persistent financial surplus. Deleveraging reduced vulnerability to interest rates and banking stress. Retained earnings and liquid assets provided a buffer against recession, supply shocks, and exchange-rate volatility. Large firms could maintain employment and investment through periods when domestic demand was weak.

    The national accounting counterpart was equally important. A government deficit can be financed domestically when other sectors save more than they invest. Japan’s nonfinancial corporations became a major source of that surplus. In FY2024, nonfinancial corporations recorded net lending equivalent to approximately 2.2 percent of nominal GDP, while the general government remained a net borrower.8 Funds did not move from a corporate treasury directly into a ministry account, but the sectoral balance closed through banks, capital markets, insurers, pensions, and foreign asset transactions.

    The corporate surplus reduced sovereign funding pressure while weakening the domestic growth mechanism. A firm retaining cash rather than investing or raising wages strengthens its own balance sheet and adds to national saving. It also reduces current household income and private capital formation. The same behavior that made the financial system safer helped entrench weak demand and low inflation.

    This was one of the old regime’s most stable exchanges:

    cautious firms
    → lower borrowing and wage pressure
    → greater private financial surplus
    → more funds available to financial intermediaries
    → easier absorption of public deficits
    → weaker domestic demand and continued need for public support.

    The state substituted for demand that private firms did not generate, while the private surplus helped finance the substitution. The loop could persist at near-zero rates because the carrying cost of the public response remained low.

    The aggregate strength also concealed heterogeneity. Large exporters accumulated cash and foreign income, while smaller domestically oriented firms operated with thinner margins and greater dependence on bank credit. The corporate buffer was national in accounting terms but uneven in its practical availability. Later chapters examine why that distinction matters once rates, wages, and import costs rise together.

    3.6 External wealth insulated the regime¶

    Japan’s external balance sheet distinguished it from conventional sovereign-debt crisis cases. Persistent current-account surpluses accumulated into a large net international investment position. By the end of 2025, Japan held 1,805.6 trillion yen of external assets against 1,243.9 trillion yen of liabilities, leaving a net position of 561.8 trillion yen.9

    The composition of the current account also changed. As Japan’s manufacturing production and investment moved abroad and energy imports increased, primary income from direct and portfolio investment became a more important source of surplus than the trade balance. Bank of Japan balance-of-payments analysis identifies investment income as a major contributor to the current account since the latter half of the 2000s.10

    This structure provided four stabilizers.

    First, Japan did not need continuing foreign-currency borrowing to finance the state. Second, overseas income supported the current account even when the goods trade balance weakened. Third, yen depreciation increased the yen valuation of foreign assets and foreign-currency income. Fourth, Japanese institutions possessed external portfolios that could be rebalanced or repatriated under stress.

    The buffer had limits that were easy to overlook. Most of the net external position was privately owned. Reinvested foreign earnings did not necessarily return as domestic wages or investment. Valuation gains caused by yen depreciation increased measured national wealth while the same depreciation reduced household purchasing power. The foreign asset position protected external solvency more effectively than it protected the median domestic consumer.

    This distinction allowed macroeconomic stability and household compression to coexist. The nation could become wealthier in yen terms as the currency weakened, even while imported necessities became more expensive and domestic real income deteriorated.

    3.7 Demography initially lowered the price of time¶

    Japan’s aging population is now treated primarily as a fiscal burden. During the construction of the old regime, its monetary effects also supported stability.

    Lower fertility and longer life expectancy reduced labor-force growth, weakened potential output, and encouraged saving for longer retirement. Firms facing weaker trend demand required less domestic capital. The resulting excess of desired saving over investment contributed to a lower natural rate of interest. Bank of Japan research estimates that demographic change accounted for about 270 basis points of the decline in Japan’s real interest rate between 1960 and 2015.11

    The demographic effect was not linear. Women and older workers entered the labor force at higher rates, slowing the decline in employment even as the working-age population contracted. Japan’s 15–64 population was approximately 73.7 million in 2024, or 59.6 percent of the total, while participation gains among women and seniors offset part of the numerical decline.12 These gains expanded the usable labor pool without reversing the age structure.

    For the old regime, demography therefore produced a temporary alignment:

    • weaker labor-force growth reduced potential growth and investment demand;
    • longer expected retirement supported precautionary saving;
    • lower natural rates made extraordinary public debt cheaper to carry;
    • rising participation delayed the point at which labor scarcity became fully binding;
    • low wage pressure reinforced the norm of weak price adjustment.

    The same alignment stored future pressure. A larger elderly population increased pension, healthcare, and long-term-care claims. Higher participation could not be repeated indefinitely once the available pool of women and older workers narrowed. Retirees eventually shifted from accumulation toward asset decumulation. Rural depopulation raised the cost of maintaining services per resident.

    Demography helped create the low-rate environment in which the public balance sheet expanded. It did not create the future revenue required to service that balance sheet once rates normalized.

    3.8 Energy and the permissive external price regime¶

    Japan has always been resource constrained, but the macroeconomic cost of that constraint varies with global prices, the exchange rate, and the domestic energy mix. For much of the old stability period, disinflation abroad, stable supply chains, and periods of yen strength limited the domestic price impact of import dependence. After the global financial crisis, weak international demand and then the 2014–2020 commodity-price environment further reduced the pressure.

    The Fukushima disaster changed the structure by reducing nuclear generation and increasing reliance on imported fossil fuels. Subsequent reactor restarts and renewable investment improved the position, but energy self-sufficiency remained only 15.3 percent in FY2023, the lowest in the Group of Seven according to the 2025 Energy White Paper.13

    Low domestic energy self-sufficiency was not a binding macro constraint when the yen and global fuel prices were favorable. It became one when both moved against Japan. The key transition was not a new physical dependence; it was the loss of a price environment that had allowed the dependence to remain politically manageable.

    Energy exposes the difference between nominal and real sovereignty. Japan can create yen and issue JGBs to subsidize electricity, gas, gasoline, or kerosene. It cannot create the imported fuel being purchased. The subsidy changes which domestic balance sheet pays the higher external price. During the old regime, the need for that transfer was intermittent. In the emerging regime, weak-yen and geopolitical exposure make it recurrent.

    3.9 Political incrementalism distributed the loss¶

    Japan’s political system did not resolve the post-bubble adjustment through a single fiscal consolidation, banking purge, or labor-market rupture. It used repeated packages, administrative guidance, credit guarantees, transfers, public works, tax adjustments, and gradual institutional reform. Policies were often criticized as too small, too late, or internally inconsistent. Their cumulative function was to prevent concentrated losses from destabilizing the system.

    Incrementalism matched the financial architecture. Long-maturity debt distributed fiscal cost across years. Relationship banking distributed corporate losses across loan renewals and restructurings. Lifetime-employment norms and bonus adjustment distributed labor cost without mass dismissal at large firms. Monetary easing distributed the sovereign cost across low asset returns and central-bank holdings. Supplementary budgets distributed shocks across future taxpayers and bondholders.

    This was not uniquely Japanese risk aversion. It was a coherent method of maintaining social and institutional continuity in an economy with weak nominal growth. Its political advantage was that no single decision appeared to impose the full adjustment. Its economic disadvantage was that low-productivity structures, regional obligations, and temporary support could survive beyond their original justification.

    The method worked best when delay was cheap. Near-zero rates made tomorrow’s fiscal cost appear small. Low inflation made nominal benefit levels and wages easier to hold. A stable currency limited the need for recurring household compensation. Once those conditions reversed, incrementalism became more expensive because every delay entered a higher-rate, higher-cost, and more distributionally contested future.

    3.10 How the stabilizers reinforced one another¶

    The old regime was durable because the buffers formed a circuit rather than a list.

    Stabilizer Immediate function Reinforcing link Deferred cost
    Corporate deleveraging Reduced private default risk Created financial surplus Weak wages and domestic demand
    Household and institutional saving Funded banks, insurers, and pensions Supported JGB demand Low returns and future decumulation
    BOJ easing and JGB purchases Lowered rates and prevented deflation Reduced public and private financing costs Market-function and exit problems
    Long debt maturity Delayed budget repricing Allowed gradual issuance adjustment Higher costs accumulated with a lag
    Fiscal expansion Replaced weak private demand Preserved employment and income Larger debt stock and recurring programs
    External assets Generated income and reduced external risk Supported current account and yen balance sheet Income weakly transmitted to households
    Low inflation norm Stabilized nominal contracts Allowed ultra-low rates Wage inertia and low price flexibility
    Political incrementalism Avoided concentrated loss Sustained institutional consent Deferred reform and embedded compensation

    The circuit explains why forecasts based on one exhausted buffer repeatedly failed. A decline in household saving did not trigger a JGB crisis because corporate saving and BOJ purchases expanded. Weak trade balances did not trigger an external crisis because primary income remained strong. Rising gross debt did not trigger a debt-service shock because coupons fell and maturities lengthened. Poor bank margins did not stop intermediation because institutions changed business models, consolidated, and retained access to central-bank liquidity.

    Japan’s resilience was adaptive. The system replaced one weakening stabilizer with another. The danger in the current regime is that several replacements are now being withdrawn or becoming costly at the same time.

    3.11 The break in alignment¶

    The first formal break came in prices. Large import shocks after 2021 pushed inflation above the ranges that had governed corporate and household behavior. Firms became more willing to pass costs forward. Labor scarcity strengthened wage demands. The Bank of Japan concluded in 2024 that the two-decade norm of wages and prices not rising was changing.14

    The second break came in monetary structure. In March 2024, the BOJ declared that negative rates and yield-curve control had fulfilled their roles and returned the short-term rate to the center of policy. By June 2026, the rate had reached 1 percent. At its July meeting the Board held that level, but one member proposed 1.25 percent, while the Bank projected core inflation clearly above 2 percent from the second half of FY2026.15

    The third break came in sovereign pricing. The effective rate on the debt stock remained low, but marginal JGB yields rose and BOJ purchases declined. Private investors were being asked to absorb more duration after years in which the central bank had dominated supply. The low-coupon inheritance still protected the budget, but it was no longer being renewed at the same price.

    The fourth break came in the distribution of nominal growth. Higher prices and corporate earnings improved tax revenue and nominal GDP. Households simultaneously faced elevated food, energy, housing, and service costs. The government responded through subsidies, transfers, and proposed tax relief. Part of the fiscal gain from inflation was therefore committed to compensating for inflation.

    The fifth break came in saving and capacity. The FY2024 household saving ratio was only 0.8 percent on the revised national-accounts basis, while nonfinancial corporations continued to run a financial surplus.16 The domestic funding circuit had not disappeared, but it depended less on a broad household surplus and more on institutions, corporate balances, and the BOJ. At the same time, labor-force participation gains were approaching practical limits and age-related service needs were rising.

    None of these changes alone ends the regime. Together they reverse its internal logic.

    Old alignment: low inflation supported low rates, low rates supported fiscal expansion, and private surpluses funded the state.
    Emerging alignment: inflation requires higher rates, higher rates reprice the state, and fiscal expansion is used to offset the inflation and credit effects of normalization.

    The old system turned weak demand into cheap public finance. The new system turns nominal improvement into a conflict over who will absorb higher prices and yields.

    3.12 What remains of the old regime¶

    Exhaustion does not mean disappearance. Japan still possesses every major institutional defense that prevented a conventional crisis.

    The debt remains overwhelmingly yen denominated. Average maturity is long. The Bank of Japan can alter purchase operations and provide liquidity. Domestic banks, insurers, and pensions retain structural demand for JGBs. The country remains a net external creditor. Corporate balance sheets are stronger than in the 1990s. The government can mobilize tax, regulatory, and administrative capacity. The financial system remains broadly resilient.

    These facts make a sudden funding stop improbable as a base case. They also shape the form of future adjustment. Japan is more likely to respond to stress through renewed central-bank purchases, issuance changes, subsidies, tax measures, regulatory incentives, and gradual balance-sheet transfers than through explicit default or abrupt austerity.

    The distinction between buffer and solution is therefore essential. A buffer reduces the probability of discontinuity. A solution restores the system’s capacity without creating an equivalent future claim. Long maturity is a buffer; it does not prevent higher coupons from entering over time. BOJ purchases are a buffer; they do not eliminate inflation or market-function costs. External assets are a buffer; they do not automatically fund the general account. Corporate cash is a buffer; it does not guarantee domestic investment or broad wage growth.

    Japan still has buffers. The old regime is exhausted because those buffers are now being asked to absorb different pressures simultaneously.

    3.13 Counterarguments and limits¶

    Three counterarguments qualify the transition.

    First, normalization could improve the regime rather than exhaust it. Positive rates can restore bank margins, improve market price discovery, reward household saving, and reduce resource misallocation. That is correct. The issue is sequencing. These long-run benefits arrive while the government refinances at higher coupons and weaker borrowers face immediate adjustment. Successful normalization requires the fiscal and private sectors to absorb the transition without forcing the BOJ back into suppression.

    Second, stronger wage and price behavior may finally create the virtuous cycle the old regime failed to produce. A durable rise in real wages, consumption, productivity, and domestic investment would broaden the tax base and reduce dependence on fiscal demand. This is the principal successful-stabilization path. It is not established by nominal wage settlements alone. The gains must extend beyond large firms and exceed the price effects transmitted through food, energy, housing, taxes, and services.

    Third, corporate and external wealth may replace declining household saving. Japan’s private sector remains wealthy and capable of holding public liabilities. The constraint is not a mechanical shortage of yen savings. It is the return required to mobilize those funds, their willingness to remain in domestic duration, and the distributional cost of using private wealth to support public borrowing rather than domestic consumption and investment.

    These counterarguments reduce the probability of a hard break. They do not re-create the alignment of the zero-rate era.

    3.14 Finding: Japan consumed the conditions that protected it¶

    The old stability regime worked because several apparent weaknesses offset one another. Corporate caution suppressed investment but created financial surplus. Demographic aging reduced potential growth but lowered the natural rate. Deflation weakened nominal demand but allowed the state to finance itself cheaply. Domestic intermediation limited market discipline but prevented an external funding stop. BOJ purchases impaired price discovery but stabilized the sovereign curve. Political delay embedded costs but prevented concentrated adjustment.

    Japan did not survive three decades by proving that debt, demography, or monetary limits were irrelevant. It survived by converting each constraint into a stabilizer for another part of the system.

    That conversion is becoming harder. Inflation has changed corporate behavior. Monetary normalization is removing the rate subsidy. Higher marginal yields are entering a debt stock accumulated under different assumptions. Household relief is consuming fiscal gains. Labor scarcity and energy imports are imposing real constraints that nominal balance-sheet operations cannot erase.

    The transition can be summarized as follows:

    managed adjustment created low inflation
    → low inflation created low rates
    → low rates financed public substitution for weak private demand
    → BOJ purchases and private surpluses stabilized the expanding debt stock
    → the resulting debt and market structure made exit increasingly consequential
    → the return of inflation forced the exit before fiscal repair was complete.

    Chapter 4 begins at that final step. It examines how the low-rate inheritance is consumed through refinancing and why the annual flow of debt service—not the gross-debt headline—is the first fiscal mechanism through which the old regime becomes a current constraint.
