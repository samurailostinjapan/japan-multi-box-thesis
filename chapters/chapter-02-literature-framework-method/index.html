    Chapter 2 — Literature, Analytical Framework, and Method¶

    Japan’s policy debate is crowded with explanations that are individually persuasive and jointly incomplete. Debt-sustainability analysis shows how a favorable relationship between nominal growth and the effective interest rate can stabilize a large debt stock. Monetary research explains how quantitative easing and yield-curve control lowered financing costs and supported activity. Demographic models show how aging reduced labor supply, potential growth, and the natural rate of interest. Exchange-rate studies trace the transmission of yen depreciation into import and consumer prices. Political-economy accounts explain why reform is incremental and compensation recurrent.

    The problem is not the absence of relevant theory. It is the tendency to hold the other constraints constant while applying it.

    Japan does not experience fiscal, monetary, currency, demographic, and political adjustment in separate compartments. A higher policy rate enters the government’s refinancing cost, bank securities portfolios, mortgage pricing, corporate credit, and the yen. A weaker yen changes import prices, corporate earnings, tax receipts, external-asset valuations, household purchasing power, and demand for subsidies. Demographic contraction lowers potential growth but can also tighten labor markets, raise wages, alter saving, and increase health and long-term-care expenditure. The causal unit is therefore not one variable. It is the policy collision created when an instrument remains effective against its immediate target but tightens a different constraint.

    This chapter constructs a framework for analyzing those collisions. It draws from established fiscal and monetary theory without treating Japan as a mechanical application of any single model. It also defines the evidentiary rules of the monograph. The objective is not to prove that Japan has entered a textbook fiscal-dominance equilibrium. It is to determine whether the practical range of monetary and fiscal choice is narrowing as a result of the public balance sheet and the institutional means used to stabilize it.

    2.1 The two inadequate poles¶

    The literature relevant to Japan is often reduced to a dispute between insolvency and monetary sovereignty.

    The insolvency position begins from the scale of gross public debt and projects a loss of investor confidence, a sharp rise in yields, and an eventual fiscal crisis. Its strength is that it takes the intertemporal budget constraint seriously. Its weakness is institutional. It frequently treats a yen-denominated sovereign with a central bank, a long maturity structure, a large domestic financial system, and substantial public and external assets as if it were a foreign-currency borrower exposed to a conventional funding stop.

    The monetary-sovereignty position begins from the state’s capacity to issue currency and settle nominal obligations. Its strength is operational. Japan cannot involuntarily run out of yen in the same manner that a household, firm, local government, or foreign-currency sovereign can run out of its settlement asset. Its weakness is economic. Creating the settlement asset does not create imported energy, labor, productive capacity, political consent, or real household income. The binding constraint may appear through inflation, exchange-rate depreciation, negative real returns, reduced central-bank credibility, capital allocation, or the distribution of losses rather than through missed nominal payments.

    Both positions therefore ask the wrong final question. The relevant issue is not whether the Japanese state can pay. It is what payment means in real and distributional terms when monetary accommodation, taxation, borrowing, and inflation are alternative methods of allocating the same underlying cost.

    The analytical middle is not a compromise between the two poles. It is a different object of study: the conditions under which financeability is maintained by narrowing the set of politically and economically tolerable choices.

    2.2 Fiscal dominance as a regime of constraint¶

    Sargent and Wallace’s “unpleasant monetarist arithmetic” established the core proposition that monetary control depends on fiscal behavior. When fiscal policy does not provide sufficient future backing for debt, a central bank that tightens today may be forced to accommodate later. Under those conditions, monetary restraint can shift inflation through time rather than eliminate the fiscal source of pressure.1

    Leeper formalized the distinction through active and passive policy regimes. An active authority sets its instrument without adjusting to the public-debt path; a passive authority responds to the constraint imposed by the other authority and the government budget. A stable equilibrium requires a coherent assignment of adjustment. When fiscal policy is active and monetary policy passive, debt disturbances affect prices because monetary policy ultimately accommodates the fiscal position.2

    That literature is essential, but Japan requires a narrower operational definition. There is no evidence at the cutoff that the Bank of Japan had surrendered formal control of its policy instrument. It ended negative rates and yield-curve control in March 2024, subsequently raised the overnight rate, and held it at 1 percent in July 2026 despite the fiscal consequences.3 Labeling every large central-bank balance sheet or every cautious rate increase “fiscal dominance” would therefore empty the term of content.

    This monograph defines operational fiscal dominance as follows:

    Operational fiscal dominance exists when the scale, maturity, and political uses of the public balance sheet materially constrain the range over which monetary policy can pursue price stability without activating fiscal, credit, financial-stability, or political stress.

    This definition differs from direct monetization. The government need not order the central bank to purchase bonds. The Bank of Japan need not abandon its inflation objective. Dominance can appear as an asymmetric reaction function: the Bank remains free to tighten, but the domestic costs of doing so rise faster than the currency or inflation benefits. Fiscal conditions then shape the pace, sequencing, and terminal extent of normalization even when the legal mandate remains intact.

    Recent literature describes fiscal dominance broadly as a condition in which monetary policy is constrained by the public sector’s budget constraint and emphasizes the strategic interaction between governments, bond markets, and central banks.4 Japan is a strong case for examining the gray zone between legal subordination and complete monetary independence. The question is empirical: whether policy choices and market responses reveal a shrinking corridor, not whether officials use the term.

    2.3 Debt sustainability under low interest rates¶

    The modern low-rate debt literature supplies the strongest counterargument to simple crisis narratives. Blanchard shows that when the safe interest rate remains below the growth rate, debt rollover can impose a far smaller fiscal burden than the gross stock suggests. A government may sustain debt without future tax increases if favorable growth-interest dynamics offset primary deficits, although welfare and risk costs remain.5

    Japan exemplified this logic. The effective rate on the debt stock declined for decades, while long maturities slowed repricing. When nominal GDP accelerated with inflation, the debt ratio could fall even as nominal debt and interest expense rose. The correct debt-dynamics equation is therefore indispensable:

    \[ \Delta d_t \approx \frac{r_t-g_t}{1+g_t}d_{t-1}-pb_t \]

    where \(d\) is the debt-to-GDP ratio, \(r\) the effective nominal interest rate, \(g\) nominal GDP growth, and \(pb\) the primary balance as a share of GDP.

    The equation is a starting point, not a sufficient model. Three qualifications matter for Japan.

    First, the relevant rate is the effective rate on the debt stock, not the current policy rate or the yield at a single maturity. The lag between marginal and effective rates creates a window in which the debt ratio improves before the budget absorbs the full cost of normalization.

    Second, nominal growth is not a free fiscal resource. Inflation raises revenue and the GDP denominator, but it also raises indexed expenditure, procurement costs, nominal wages, social transfers, and political demands for relief. The fiscal value of inflation depends on the speed with which revenue, spending, and coupons reprice.

    Third, \(r-g\) is endogenous to the policy response. Growth spending may raise potential output, but unfunded spending can also increase the term premium. Rate suppression may preserve favorable debt dynamics, but it can weaken the yen and raise imported inflation. Tightening may improve price credibility, but it raises the marginal rate that later enters \(r\).

    The IMF’s 2026 Japan assessment captures the temporal issue. It projected that favorable growth-interest dynamics would continue lowering the gross-debt ratio for a period, while refinancing and age-related spending would eventually push the ratio upward from the mid-2030s.6 The Ministry of Finance’s own sensitivity analysis reaches the same mechanism from the budget side: national debt service accelerates as higher rates pass through the maturity structure. The monograph therefore treats low-rate debt sustainability as conditional and path dependent, not as either proof of safety or evidence of inevitable crisis.

    2.4 Financial repression and the price of stability¶

    Financial-repression literature explains how states reduce the real burden of domestic-currency debt without formal default. Reinhart and Sbrancia identify a historical combination of regulated financial systems, captive or encouraged domestic holders, controlled nominal rates, and moderate inflation that produced negative real returns and transferred resources from savers to sovereign borrowers.7

    Japan does not map neatly onto the postwar cases. Its financial system is liberalized, capital is mobile, and JGB holdings arise from a mixture of regulatory requirements, liability matching, liquidity management, portfolio choice, and central-bank operations. “Captive buyer” is therefore too crude a description of banks, insurers, and pension funds. These institutions hold government bonds for reasons that remain economically valid even without political direction.

    The repression lens is nevertheless useful because it forces attention onto distribution. Yield-curve control reduced the state’s funding cost and supported monetary transmission. It also compressed returns available to savers and financial institutions, altered price discovery, reduced the free float of bonds, and made the central bank a dominant participant in the sovereign market. The Bank of Japan’s own research finds that purchases can support transaction volume through flow effects, while a high stock of BOJ holdings and continuous fixed-rate operations can reduce trading and distort the yield curve.8

    The relevant question is not whether Japan practiced “pure” financial repression. It is whether the old regime preserved fiscal and macroeconomic stability partly by holding the return on safe domestic assets below the rate that a less managed market would have required. If so, normalization is not merely a change in monetary stance. It is the gradual removal of a fiscal subsidy embedded in the yield curve.

    2.5 Japan-specific explanations of low JGB yields¶

    Japan’s bond-market literature has long treated low yields as a puzzle. Hoshi and Ito examined why JGB prices remained high despite an apparently unsustainable fiscal trajectory and focused on the domestic savings pool, home bias, financial intermediation, and the possibility that aging would eventually weaken those supports.9 Their work is important less as a dated prediction than as a statement of the old regime’s balance-sheet logic: private domestic surpluses funded the public deficit through institutions willing or required to hold yen assets.

    Subsequent events modified that mechanism. The Bank of Japan replaced a substantial share of private demand and came to hold an extraordinary portion of outstanding JGBs. The current investor base is therefore not the pre-QQE investor base. Domestic institutions remain central, but the BOJ’s stock effect, purchase schedule, and exit path now influence the quantity and price of duration that private investors must absorb.

    Japan’s public and external assets provide a second line of argument. Gross debt overstates the consolidated public burden when the state owns financial assets, and Japan’s net international investment position substantially reduces external risk. Those observations are correct. The analytical error is to consolidate assets across institutions without preserving their mandates, liquidity, and ownership. Pension assets, fiscal-loan assets, foreign reserves, and privately owned overseas portfolios cannot all be applied to the general account without offsetting liabilities or policy costs.

    The monograph therefore uses three balance-sheet measures for different purposes:

    Measure Analytical use Main limitation
    Gross public debt Scale of obligations and refinancing Ignores public assets and intragovernmental positions
    Net public debt Consolidated public balance-sheet exposure Depends on asset definition, liquidity, and statistical boundary
    Net international investment position External solvency and income capacity Mostly private; not general-account fiscal space

    No single measure dominates. The correct measure depends on the mechanism being tested.

    2.6 Demography, saving, and the natural rate¶

    Demographic research adds both a stabilizer and a destabilizer to the Japanese case. A lower working-age growth rate reduces potential output and the tax base. Greater longevity and a larger elderly share increase pension, health, and long-term-care spending. At the same time, preparation for retirement and lower investment demand can increase desired saving relative to domestic capital formation, reducing the natural rate of interest and supporting low government borrowing costs.

    Bank of Japan research using an overlapping-generations model estimated that demographic change accounted for roughly 270 basis points of the decline in Japan’s real interest rate from 1960 to 2015. The authors also found that the additional effect of future demographic change on the rate would be smaller than the inherited effect of past fertility and longevity shifts.10 IMF research similarly associates aging and a shrinking population with a lower natural rate through weaker potential growth.11

    This is a critical complication. Demographic decline does not mechanically produce rising JGB yields. It can suppress the equilibrium rate even as it worsens the fiscal balance. That combination helped sustain the old regime: low labor-force growth weakened real expansion, but low natural rates reduced the cost of carrying debt.

    The relationship changes as households move from accumulation to decumulation, pension funds pay benefits, labor scarcity raises service costs, and the government expands age-related spending. The 2026 survey literature on Japanese demographics and fiscal sustainability emphasizes precisely this interaction among social insurance, household responses, participation, migration, and family formation.12 The fiscal effect cannot be inferred from the population count alone.

    The monograph therefore treats demography as a rate-, flow-, and capacity variable:

    • a rate variable through its effect on the natural interest rate and wage pressure;
    • a flow variable through taxes, benefits, saving, and asset decumulation;
    • a capacity variable through labor availability, care provision, regional infrastructure, and the ability to execute public and private investment.

    2.7 The yen, pass-through, and distribution¶

    Exchange-rate literature explains why yen depreciation can support aggregate income while reducing domestic welfare. The pass-through begins at the border. Foreign-currency invoicing transmits depreciation into yen import prices, while firms then decide how much of the higher cost to absorb in margins or pass into domestic prices. Empirical work on Japan finds substantial pass-through into import prices but weaker and more conditional transmission into consumer prices.13

    That weaker consumer-price pass-through was a stabilizer during the deflationary regime. Firms compressed margins, changed sourcing, delayed repricing, or relied on productivity and wage restraint rather than fully transmitting import costs. It also meant that depreciation could improve exporter earnings and raise inflation modestly without immediately producing a generalized household-price shock.

    The relationship is nonlinear. Bank of Japan research finds that pass-through from producer prices, exchange rates, and wages rises after cost increases cross estimated thresholds; linear models can understate inflation when shocks are large.14 The 2020s therefore cannot be analyzed by applying average pass-through coefficients from the low-inflation period. Once firms’ price-setting behavior changes, a given yen movement can generate a different domestic response.

    The distributional channels are equally important. Yen weakness:

    • increases the yen value of overseas earnings and foreign assets;
    • can improve profits at globally exposed firms;
    • supports nominal tax revenue and primary-income receipts;
    • raises the cost of imported energy, food, materials, and transport;
    • harms households and domestically oriented firms without matching foreign income;
    • creates fiscal pressure for subsidies and tax relief.

    The yen is therefore not merely an output of the framework. It is a pressure valve through which unresolved monetary-fiscal tension enters the domestic political economy.

    2.8 Institutional resilience and incremental adjustment¶

    Japan-specific political and financial literature stresses institutional persistence. The post-bubble adjustment was slowed by delayed bank recapitalization, incomplete balance-sheet repair, and gradual structural reform, but those same institutions also prevented disorderly liquidation.15 Policy moved through negotiated, sequential adjustments rather than a single clearing event.

    This pattern matters because slow adjustment is not synonymous with policy failure. Extending credit, protecting employment, recapitalizing institutions gradually, and using fiscal demand can prevent a balance-sheet recession from becoming a depression. The cost is that weak firms, low-return capital, regional obligations, and temporary programs can remain embedded in the system after the original shock has passed.

    The political economy of compression is therefore built around compensation rather than resolution. A policy with concentrated losses and diffuse future benefits is difficult to sustain. Relief measures reverse that distribution: they provide immediate visible benefits while placing costs on future budgets, savers, the currency, or the central-bank balance sheet. Repeated use makes the fiscal system more responsive to current prices and less capable of committing future resources independently.

    This is the institutional bridge between economic mechanism and policy behavior. The state does not ignore the constraint. It continuously reallocates it.

    2.9 The literature gap¶

    The relevant literatures do not fail because they are wrong. They fail as a complete account because each tends to specify the variables governed by another field as assumptions.

    • Debt dynamics often take the interest rate, primary balance, and growth path as externally given.
    • Monetary analysis often treats fiscal backing and political tolerance as background conditions.
    • JGB-market analysis studies holder behavior without fully modeling household compensation or exchange-rate pass-through.
    • Demographic models frequently abstract from short-run currency and market shocks.
    • exchange-rate studies estimate price transmission without integrating the fiscal response to household losses.
    • Political analysis explains delayed reform without quantifying the refinancing and rate mechanisms that change its cost.

    The gap is a framework for endogenous collision: a method for tracing how the response inside one domain changes the state variables of another.

    Slow compression fills that gap at the level of applied political economy. It is not offered as a general-equilibrium model. It is a disciplined causal map used to organize empirical evidence, arithmetic, and event analysis across institutions that cannot be meaningfully separated in the Japanese case.

    2.10 The multi-box framework¶

    The framework contains eight primary constraint boxes.

    Box Core state variable Immediate pressure Typical response Collision created
    Fiscal Primary balance, debt service, rollover Rising committed expenditure Borrowing, tax change, asset income Higher yields or reduced future capacity
    Monetary Inflation, policy rate, BOJ balance sheet Price persistence and credibility Rate hikes, purchase reduction Fiscal, credit, and bank stress
    JGB/financial Term premium, investor base, liquidity Greater private duration absorption Higher yields, issuance changes, BOJ support Budget repricing or renewed market distortion
    Currency USD/JPY and broad yen valuation Rate differential and policy credibility Intervention or monetary tightening Reserve use or domestic tightening
    Household Real income, consumption, saving Food, energy, housing, and tax burden Subsidies, transfers, wage policy Larger fiscal claim
    Corporate/SME Margins, cash, credit, investment Wage, input, and financing costs Price pass-through, borrowing, consolidation Inflation, employment, or bank losses
    Demographic/energy Labor, dependency, import exposure Capacity and terms-of-trade shock Automation, migration, subsidies, strategic investment High transition cost and political resistance
    Political Approval, coalition stability, distribution Low tolerance for concentrated loss Delay, compensation, administrative control Credibility erosion and recurring expenditure

    The boxes are analytical partitions, not independent sectors. A single event may enter several simultaneously. An oil shock affects the energy, currency, household, corporate, fiscal, and monetary boxes. A JGB selloff affects the financial, fiscal, monetary, and political boxes. The framework is designed to prevent an analyst from declaring the event resolved when its price has merely moved.

    Four transmission concepts govern the map.

    Pressure valve: the variable allowed to move because other variables are being controlled. Under strong yield suppression, the yen can become the pressure valve. Under currency defense, domestic rates or reserve assets may absorb the adjustment.

    Stress transfer: a policy shifts the payer without reducing the real cost. An energy subsidy transfers part of an import shock from households to the budget.

    Solution collision: an intervention improves its target while worsening another constraint. A rate hike can reduce inflation pressure while accelerating debt-service and credit stress.

    Timeline pull-forward: an event causes a constraint expected later to become relevant earlier. Persistently high marginal yields can pull the refinancing window forward even though the average debt rate remains low.

    2.11 From framework to testable propositions¶

    The framework produces observable expectations.

    If fiscal dominance is strengthening, monetary normalization should become more sensitive to debt-service, bank, and credit transmission even when inflation remains above target. If the yen is functioning as a pressure valve, rate moves insufficient to alter the monetary-fiscal mix should produce only temporary currency relief. If political compensation is structural, initial budgets should repeatedly understate the realized fiscal stance. If domestic JGB absorption is weakening at the margin, auctions may continue to clear while requiring higher concessions or changes in issuance composition. If household endurance is a binding constraint, improvements in nominal revenue should be followed by subsidies or tax relief rather than durable primary-balance improvement.

    These expectations do not require simultaneous deterioration in every indicator. They require correlated policy responses consistent with stress transfer.

    2.12 Research design¶

    The monograph uses a mixed qualitative-quantitative case-study design.

    Historical institutional analysis reconstructs the post-bubble stability regime and identifies the institutional functions performed by low rates, domestic intermediaries, the BOJ, corporate saving, and external assets.

    Process tracing follows defined episodes from shock through policy response and secondary effects. The monetary normalization sequence, energy-subsidy extensions, supplementary budgets, consumption-tax proposals, and foreign-exchange interventions are treated as causal episodes rather than isolated headlines.

    Fiscal arithmetic separates the debt stock from annual financing flows. It distinguishes interest from total debt service, net borrowing from gross issuance, initial from supplementary budgets, and the marginal interest rate from the effective rate on outstanding debt.

    Market event analysis compares changes across the policy-rate curve, JGB term structure, USD/JPY, yen crosses, and official communications. These episodes are not used to infer long-run causality from a single trading session. They identify how markets price the interaction among policy instruments.

    Structured scenarios vary rates, growth, fiscal response, energy conditions, and investor behavior. A baseline is not designated “true”; it is the path requiring the fewest additional shocks. Stress cases expose nonlinearities and policy ceilings.

    The design is intentionally mechanism centered. Regression estimates from the literature are used where they clarify a transmission channel, but the monograph does not claim to estimate a new structural macroeconomic model.

    2.13 Evidence hierarchy and classification¶

    Sources are ranked by their proximity to the claim.

    1. Primary Japanese sources: Ministry of Finance budgets and debt reports; Bank of Japan policy decisions, flow-of-funds data, outlooks, and research; Cabinet Office national accounts and projections; Statistics Bureau and population-institute data; Diet and ministry documents.
    2. International institutions: IMF, OECD, BIS, World Bank, and IEA analysis used for comparable definitions, external assessment, and specialized datasets.
    3. Academic research: peer-reviewed articles and working papers used to establish mechanisms, estimation results, and competing interpretations.
    4. Market and corporate sources: auction data, filings, bank disclosures, and industry statistics.
    5. High-quality reporting: used for event timing, political commitments, statements, and market reaction when the primary record is incomplete at the cutoff.

    Each substantive claim is assigned one of three statuses during drafting:

    Observed fact — reported or directly calculable from released evidence.
    Mechanism-based inference — a causal interpretation consistent with the evidence.
    Stress scenario — a conditional result based on explicit assumptions.

    The labels need not appear beside every sentence in the final prose. The distinction must remain recoverable from the wording and notes. “MOF projects” is not “will occur.” “The market priced” is not “the market knows.” “The monograph’s stress path” is not an official forecast.

    2.14 Measurement rules¶

    Japan’s fiscal and financial data create recurring category errors. The following rules apply throughout:

    • “Debt service” includes budgeted interest and redemption transfers unless explicitly limited to interest expenditure.
    • “Gross issuance” includes refunding bonds and is not treated as new borrowing.
    • “Government debt” preserves the source’s statistical boundary; national government general bonds, central-and-local debt, and general-government gross debt are not spliced into one series.
    • “Domestic ownership” distinguishes the BOJ from private domestic investors.
    • “Inflation” identifies the index and subsidy treatment where material.
    • “Wage growth” distinguishes nominal from real wages and large-firm settlements from economy-wide outcomes.
    • “Current account” distinguishes trade in goods and services from primary income.
    • “Intervention” remains unconfirmed until official data identify the amount or date; market evidence may support only a probability judgment.

    These rules are not editorial detail. The argument depends on preserving the exact location of the pressure.

    2.15 Counterarguments and falsification¶

    The framework can become unfalsifiable if every stabilizing event is described as temporary and every adverse event as confirmation. This monograph rejects that construction.

    The thesis is weakened if Japan produces a durable combination of the following outcomes:

    • broad real-wage growth across large firms and SMEs;
    • private domestic demand that expands without repeated fiscal transfers;
    • a structural reduction in supplementary budgets;
    • sustained primary-balance improvement after temporary inflation gains fade;
    • debt service that stabilizes relative to tax revenue despite monetary normalization;
    • a yen that stabilizes without repeated intervention or renewed yield suppression;
    • orderly private absorption of JGB supply with a stable term premium;
    • productivity growth sufficient to offset labor-force contraction and transition costs;
    • entitlement and tax reform that preserves social protection while reducing the unfunded path.

    The thesis is strengthened when relief in one variable reliably appears as deterioration in another—for example, lower measured energy inflation accompanied by higher fiscal expenditure, or stronger anti-inflation policy accompanied by a disproportionate increase in refinancing and credit stress.

    Several limitations remain. Event studies cannot fully separate intervention, positioning, and contemporaneous news. Official long-run projections are highly sensitive to assumptions. Cross-sector balance sheets contain valuation effects that can be mistaken for spendable income. The evidence cutoff captures a regime in motion, so subsequent policy can alter the path. These limits argue for conditional findings, not analytical agnosticism.

    2.16 Finding: interaction is the missing variable¶

    The literature provides no basis for describing Japan as either insolvent or unconstrained. It provides a stronger conclusion: the country’s unusual stability resulted from a particular alignment of fiscal arithmetic, low natural rates, domestic intermediation, central-bank balance-sheet policy, external wealth, corporate saving, and incremental political adjustment.

    That alignment made an enormous debt stock financeable at low annual cost. It did not abolish the cost. It distributed the cost through low returns, weak wage and price growth, corporate caution, central-bank market absorption, future fiscal claims, and household adjustment.

    The multi-box framework treats the erosion of that alignment as an empirical question. Its test is not whether Japan experiences a dramatic break. Its test is whether maintaining continuity requires increasingly frequent transfers of pressure among the budget, the BOJ, the yen, domestic credit, and household purchasing power.

    Chapter 3 reconstructs the old alignment in detail. Before the monograph can explain why the corridor is narrowing, it must explain why it remained wide enough for so long.
