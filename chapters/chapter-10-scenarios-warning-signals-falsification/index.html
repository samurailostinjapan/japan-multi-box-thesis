    Chapter 10 — Scenarios, Warning Signals, and Falsification¶

    The multi-box framework is useful only if it can distinguish among plausible futures before the outcome is known. A framework that explains every development after the fact explains very little. Japan's institutional resilience makes this discipline especially important. The government can refinance in its own currency, the Bank of Japan can alter the quantity and maturity of its bond purchases, the Ministry of Finance can change the issuance calendar and intervene in foreign exchange, banks can carry losses through time, and the political system can transfer private pressure to the public balance sheet. These capacities prevent a single indicator from determining the path.

    This chapter therefore does not forecast one terminal event. It defines four conditional paths from the position observed on July 31, 2026: managed compression, market-enforced adjustment, external-shock acceleration, and successful stabilization. The scenarios are not equally likely, nor are they fully mutually exclusive. An energy shock can move the system from managed compression toward market-enforced adjustment. A recession can relieve the currency and inflation boxes while worsening the fiscal and demographic ones. Successful stabilization can begin inside the managed-compression path if several temporary buffers are converted into durable capacity.

    The base case remains managed compression. Japan continues to finance itself, avoid a generalized banking crisis, and use fiscal and administrative instruments to prevent concentrated losses from becoming an acute national rupture. Debt service rises, the yen remains structurally weak, supplementary budgets recur, and real household gains remain uneven. The system does not fail. It becomes more expensive to stabilize.

    That conclusion is provisional. The chapter specifies the evidence that would move the analysis toward another scenario and the conditions that would weaken or reject the monograph's central thesis. The objective is not to defend a date or an exchange-rate target. It is to identify when Japan's feasible policy set is narrowing, when markets are beginning to enforce adjustment, and when the policy corridor is genuinely widening.

    10.1 Scenarios are conditional paths, not forecasts with false precision¶

    A scenario begins with a causal sequence. It identifies the starting configuration, the shock or policy change, the balance sheets through which the change is transmitted, the institutional response, and the conditions under which the sequence either stabilizes or changes regime. A list of adverse events is not a scenario. Neither is a straight-line extrapolation from the latest market price.

    The four paths use the distinctions established in Chapter 2.

    Analytical category Meaning in this chapter Example
    Observed fact Measured condition at or before the evidence cutoff FY2026 national debt service was budgeted at 31.3 trillion yen
    Mechanism-based inference Direction implied by institutional transmission Persistently higher issuance yields will raise the effective interest rate with a lag
    Stress scenario Conditional outcome under specified assumptions Debt service approaches 50 trillion yen around FY2029–FY2030 if rates remain well above the official baseline
    Regime trigger Combination indicating that the feasible policy set has changed Rising long yields and yen depreciation persist together despite intervention and expected BOJ tightening
    Falsification evidence Sustained joint improvement inconsistent with compression Broad real wages, private demand, the primary balance, JGB demand, and currency stability improve together after temporary support expires

    This classification prevents three common errors.

    The first is level determinism. USD/JPY at 165, a 10-year JGB yield at 3 percent, or a policy rate at 1.5 percent can be politically and financially important. None is a self-executing crisis threshold. The same level can reflect orderly normalization, an inflation shock, global repricing, fiscal-risk premium, or thin liquidity. Persistence and joint movement determine its meaning.

    The second is baseline reification. Official projections are conditional models. The Cabinet Office's higher-growth cases assume substantial gains in total factor productivity, investment, and labor participation. The Ministry of Finance's debt-service path assumes specified nominal growth and interest-rate trajectories. The BOJ's outlook assumes a future path for oil prices, government measures, wages, and global demand. These documents reveal the arithmetic required for stabilization; they do not guarantee that the assumptions will be realized.1

    The third is one-variable falsification. A falling gross-debt ratio does not disprove compression if debt service, household support, and term premiums rise. A weak month of consumption does not prove it. The framework concerns compatibility across several constraints. Confirmation and rejection must therefore be based on joint configurations.

    The scenarios are ranked qualitatively rather than assigned point probabilities. The evidence does not support a statistically estimated probability distribution through 2045. At the cutoff, managed compression is the dominant path; market-enforced adjustment is a material transition risk; external shock is an accelerant whose probability and direction depend on the event; successful stabilization is a lower-likelihood but economically coherent alternative. Those rankings must change when the evidence changes.

    10.2 The starting configuration at the July 2026 cutoff¶

    Scenario analysis must begin from the actual operating system, not from the simplified image of a country with high debt and low rates.

    The fiscal starting point combined substantial resilience with a visible refinancing path. FY2026 national debt service was 31.3 trillion yen. MOF projected 41.3 trillion yen in FY2029 under its 3-percent nominal-growth case and 40.3 trillion yen under its 1.5-percent case. Its published sensitivity reached 48.9 trillion yen when rates ran two percentage points above the first baseline. The approximately 50-trillion-yen figure used in this monograph is therefore a stress path derived from official sensitivity analysis, not an official forecast.2

    The longer projection was already bifurcating. In the Cabinet Office's current-trend case, the ratio of central and local government bonds to GDP stopped falling around 2030 and rose thereafter. Its stronger cases required productivity and participation gains large enough to preserve nominal growth and primary improvement. The IMF reached a similar directional conclusion using a different debt boundary: favorable interest-growth dynamics reduced the ratio during the near term, but interest and age-related spending turned it upward from approximately 2035.3

    The monetary starting point was a 1-percent policy rate, negative real rates in much of the curve, and unfinished normalization. The BOJ judged financial conditions accommodative and the financial system stable, while warning that underlying inflation could exceed 2 percent. The estimated nominal neutral-rate range of approximately 1.1–2.5 percent remained wide. The monograph's 1.25–1.5 percent practical compression zone was a hypothesis about fiscal, credit, housing, and political absorption—not an official ceiling.4

    The bond market had moved far beyond the old regime. Around the cutoff, the 10-year yield was testing the 2.9–3.0 percent area and the 30-year sector had traded above 4 percent. Those levels had not prevented auctions from clearing or caused a generalized liquidity event. They had changed the marginal cost at which the government refinanced and exposed weaker private demand in parts of the super-long sector. The BOJ's May survey still reported a negative diffusion index for market functioning, while market participants described improving price discovery alongside incomplete liquidity and unstable super-long demand.5

    The currency was functioning as the pressure valve. USD/JPY had reached 163.99 before a sharp July 30 move to 158.34, then retraced toward 160 after the BOJ held. The exact amount of the suspected July 30–31 intervention remained unconfirmed at the cutoff, but the earlier April–May campaign was official: MOF spent 11.7349 trillion yen and obtained a substantial but temporary appreciation. The episode demonstrated both remaining state capacity and intervention decay.6

    Domestic conditions were mixed rather than uniformly weak. May 2026 real cash earnings rose 1.4 percent, the fifth consecutive positive month, but real consumption expenditure among two-or-more-person households fell 0.4 percent from a year earlier. SME cost pass-through improved to 54.2 percent, leaving a material share of cost increases to be absorbed elsewhere. Bank lending remained active, capital exceeded regulatory requirements, and the BOJ's stress tests did not indicate imminent system-wide instability. Failures and financing pressure were concentrated in the vulnerable tail.7

    The political regime was already converting those pressures into public claims. FY2026 had received a 3.1135-trillion-yen first supplementary budget financed entirely with special deficit-financing bonds. Energy support continued to be adjusted, and a two-year reduction of the consumption tax on food had been announced for FY2027 but not enacted. From FY2016 through FY2023, bonds had financed 74.5 percent of supplementary-budget resources in the Board of Audit's review.8

    These facts define a system inside its feasible set. Financing worked. The BOJ could still tighten. Banks could absorb stress. Households held large assets. The state retained reserve and borrowing capacity. The warning was not incapacity. It was the number of stabilizers already being used at the same time.

    10.3 Three horizons: 2027–2030, 2030–2035, and 2035–2045¶

    The scenarios unfold across three clocks. They should not be compressed into a single crisis date.

    10.3.1 2027–2030: refinancing becomes politically visible¶

    The first horizon is dominated by policy collision. Bonds issued during the higher-yield regime enter the stock, the BOJ tests the practical compression zone, private investors absorb more marginal duration, and weak-yen inflation continues to generate demands for relief. The announced food-tax reduction, if enacted, begins in April 2027. Its scheduled restoration after two years would itself become a political event. Energy support, disaster reconstruction, defense, industrial policy, and ordinary aging costs compete with a debt-service line rising by several trillion yen per year.

    This is the most plausible period for an observable transition from policy-controlled stability to market-driven constraint. The transition does not require a failed auction. It can appear through persistent concessions at successful auctions, an issuance shift toward shorter maturities, renewed BOJ purchases described as market-function operations, a currency that remains weak despite rate increases, or fiscal packages whose financing becomes the dominant market question.

    The 50-trillion-yen debt-service stress result belongs to this horizon because the official high-rate sensitivity reaches 48.9 trillion yen in FY2029. It is not a prediction that 50 trillion yen will be paid. The trigger is whether the rate structure and political budget keep the realized path near that sensitivity after temporary offsets are removed.

    10.3.2 2030–2035: balance-sheet selection replaces universal accommodation¶

    The second horizon is less about the first rate shock than about accumulated repricing. A greater portion of public debt carries higher coupons. Mortgages, SME loans, and bank portfolios have had time to reset. Firms that survived through cash buffers or repeated rollover face a more discriminating credit environment. Regional institutions confront the divergence between higher asset yields and weaker local borrowers.

    Japan can remain financially stable through this selection. Bank consolidation, business exit, municipal coordination, and labor reallocation may improve productivity. The risk is that selection occurs faster than replacement capacity develops. In a shrinking region, the exit of a low-productivity transport operator, construction firm, clinic, food processor, or retailer does not automatically produce a more productive successor. It can remove the service.

    The fiscal distinction also sharpens. Nominal growth may continue to lower the debt ratio, but the effective interest rate catches up as older securities mature. The IMF's baseline places the later turn near 2035; the Cabinet Office's current-trend case turns earlier. The exact year matters less than the observable change in the snowball effect: if nominal growth no longer exceeds the effective rate by enough to offset the primary balance, fiscal stabilization requires explicit policy rather than inherited coupons.9

    10.3.3 2035–2045: demography removes recovery options¶

    The third horizon is governed increasingly by capacity. The workforce is smaller, the older population is larger, care needs are more intensive, and regional infrastructure must be maintained across a thinner user base. The people available to work in 2040 are substantially determined by cohorts already alive. Fertility improvement in the late 2020s matters greatly for the second half of the century and little for the 2030 workforce.

    Demography does not dictate a sovereign event. It determines how much labor, tax revenue, household saving, institutional staffing, and political tolerance are available to recover from other shocks. Higher productivity, later work, migration, automation, and service consolidation can offset part of the loss. The Cabinet Office's strongest case illustrates the scale required: total factor productivity rises from 0.3 percent in FY2025 to 1.1 percent within roughly five years and eventually 1.4 percent, while participation reaches 68.3 percent by FY2040. Its current-trend case holds TFP near 0.6 percent and participation near 66.5 percent.10

    The long horizon therefore tests implementation rather than announcement. Appropriating funds for AI, care technology, grids, defense, or regional infrastructure is not equivalent to producing capacity. By the 2030s, the distinction between financial authorization and real execution becomes one of the system's binding variables.

    10.4 Scenario A — Managed compression¶

    Managed compression is the base case because it requires the least departure from observed institutional behavior. Authorities continue to respond incrementally, distribute losses, and use the long refinancing lag. Markets demand more compensation but do not refuse yen financing. The BOJ normalizes gradually, intervenes or threatens intervention when currency moves become disorderly, and adjusts bond operations when liquidity deteriorates. The government continues to use supplementary budgets and targeted price relief rather than imposing a large, front-loaded fiscal adjustment.

    The path has six defining features.

    First, the BOJ raises rates gradually but stops short of unambiguously restrictive territory. The policy rate moves through the 1.25–1.5 percent practical compression zone, possibly higher if broad wages and credit prove resilient. Each increase is separated by an absorption period. The Bank continues to distinguish overnight-rate policy from long-bond market-function operations. Real rates remain low enough to avoid a severe domestic-demand contraction.

    The currency effect depends on the relative path. If foreign central banks tighten at the same time, part of the BOJ increase is nullified through the unchanged or widening expected rate differential. This synchronized-hike nullification does not mean the Japanese increase has no effect; it means Japan can incur the domestic cost of tightening without receiving the currency support implied by a Japan-only comparison.

    Second, the yen remains weak and volatile rather than entering uncontrolled depreciation. USD/JPY can trade through 165 and occasionally test 170, but official action, global rate changes, positioning, and a cautious BOJ prevent a continuous one-way move. The 165 area is an instability zone and 170 a policy-confrontation zone in this framework, not an official target or guaranteed intervention level. The key signature is repeated retracement rather than permanent stabilization.

    Third, the JGB market clears at higher yields. The 10-year and super-long sectors embed more inflation, supply, and term premium. Auctions remain covered, but successful bidders require larger concessions and private portfolios adjust duration. The BOJ's stock holdings decline slowly enough to avoid disorder, while the Ministry of Finance modifies issuance at the margin to meet investor demand. The FY2026 issuance plan already placed total JGB issuance at 180.7 trillion yen; stability depends on predictable management of that flow rather than a return to zero yields.11

    Fourth, fiscal relief becomes a recurring layer. Supplementary budgets respond to energy, disasters, strategic industry, and household pressure. Some measures are brought into the initial budget, but the combined annual stance remains larger than the initial plan suggests. Revenue windfalls and prior-year issuance reductions are used to claim that new measures do not increase current market supply, even though the underlying commitments rise.

    Fifth, aggregate domestic resilience coexists with a widening vulnerable tail. Large firms retain pricing power, foreign earnings, and access to capital. Banks benefit initially from wider margins. Real wages produce intermittent gains. SMEs with weak balance sheets exit, consolidate, or reduce investment. Households protect cash flow by restraining discretionary consumption. The state prevents these losses from synchronizing into a national contraction.

    Sixth, demographic adaptation remains sufficient to prevent breakdown and insufficient to restore broad fiscal room. Older workers, women, and foreign labor support the workforce. Automation offsets selected tasks. Metropolitan regions continue to attract labor and capital. Rural service networks thin, and public resources are used to preserve minimum capacity.

    The causal sequence is therefore:

    higher inflation and yields
    → gradual BOJ normalization and higher refinancing cost
    → persistent yen and household pressure
    → targeted relief and supplementary financing
    → continued JGB absorption at a higher term premium
    → slower domestic demand and selective private exit
    → no acute rupture, but a narrower corridor for the next shock.

    Managed compression is not a static equilibrium. It consumes buffers. Its durability depends on whether nominal income, private saving, bank profitability, and institutional demand replenish those buffers as quickly as higher debt service, relief spending, and demographic costs use them.

    The scenario remains intact when intervention works for months rather than permanently, auctions require higher yields but clear without repeated disorder, bank stress remains concentrated, real wages occasionally outpace prices without generating strong consumption, and the government finances each package without an explicit loss of market access. It begins to migrate toward Scenario B when several of those stabilizers lose effectiveness together. It moves toward Scenario D when private wages, productivity, demand, and fiscal balances improve after support is withdrawn.

    10.5 Scenario B — Market-enforced adjustment¶

    Market-enforced adjustment does not mean that investors dictate a budget to the Diet or that Japan loses the ability to issue yen debt. It means that market prices, auction behavior, and portfolio demand constrain the timing and composition of policy faster than the political system would otherwise choose.

    The regime change begins with a joint configuration, not one headline yield.

    1. Long JGB yields rise because private buyers demand more compensation for duration, inflation, or supply.
    2. The yen remains weak or weakens further, showing that higher long yields are not being interpreted as a sufficiently tighter monetary regime.
    3. MOF intervention generates a shorter-lived appreciation, while the BOJ faces pressure to raise the policy rate faster.
    4. Faster tightening raises the cost of refinancing, mortgages, and SME loans; bond purchases intended to contain the long end weaken the currency signal.
    5. The government compensates households or vulnerable firms, reinforcing issuance and fiscal-risk concerns.
    6. Banks and insurers reduce duration or demand more yield, making the state more dependent on BOJ operations or shorter issuance.

    This is the solution-collision spiral in market form.

    The first visible evidence would likely appear in auction quality, not an outright failure. Bid-to-cover ratios must be compared with the same maturity's history because a ratio can remain high while price concessions widen. The auction tail—the gap between average and lowest accepted prices—shows how much yield was required to place the marginal bonds. Repeated deterioration across several auctions matters more than a single poor sale. A growing dependence on short maturities may reduce current coupon cost or respond to buyer demand, but it raises rollover frequency and exposes the budget more quickly to future rate changes.

    The second signal would be simultaneous yen weakness and rising long yields. Under orderly normalization, higher Japanese yields should support the currency at the margin. When USD/JPY and long yields rise together for weeks, markets may be pricing domestic funding pressure without believing that the BOJ can tighten enough to close the policy gap. The July 2026 monitoring bands—roughly 164–165 on USD/JPY, 2.9–3.0 percent on the 10-year, and 4 percent on the 30-year—are useful reference zones because they were already being tested. They are not fresh thresholds. Persistence above them after policy action is the information.12

    A sustained move toward 180 on USD/JPY would be politically toxic and likely force an extraordinary response, but it is too distant to function as an early-warning threshold. By that point, the useful evidence would already have appeared in intervention frequency, imported inflation, household compensation, and the joint behavior of the yen and JGB curve.

    The third signal would be conflicted BOJ operations. The Bank can raise the overnight rate while purchasing long bonds to preserve market functioning. That is a legitimate separation of tools. The operation becomes evidence of compression if purchase increases are repeated while underlying inflation remains above target and the currency weakens, or if the planned taper is slowed primarily because fiscal supply and yields have become politically intolerable. Market participants already warned in May that slowing purchase reductions could be read as an attempt to curb long rates, while super-long demand remained unstable.13

    The fourth signal would be credit selection spreading beyond the vulnerable tail. The April 2026 BOJ stress tests showed capital ratios remaining above regulatory requirements even after a one-percentage-point parallel rise in yen rates, and under severe global and geopolitical stress. That is strong counterevidence to an imminent banking crisis. Scenario B requires more than valuation losses. It requires rising credit costs, lending retrenchment, deposit competition, or duration losses to change behavior across a meaningful set of regional and cooperative institutions.14

    Market enforcement can produce several policy responses short of a conventional crisis:

    • a faster BOJ rate path combined with targeted liquidity operations;
    • heavier JGB purchases or fixed-rate operations in selected maturities;
    • shorter issuance and reductions in super-long supply;
    • stronger regulatory incentives for domestic institutions to hold sovereign debt;
    • tax-advantaged retail JGB programs;
    • budget offsets, benefit restraint, or withdrawal of subsidies;
    • consolidation or public support for vulnerable financial institutions;
    • measures that hold domestic returns below inflation and thereby deepen financial repression.

    These instruments can stabilize the market. Their use would confirm that adjustment had become market-enforced if they were adopted earlier or on worse terms than policymakers had planned.

    Scenario B still does not imply default. Japan's currency denomination, BOJ capacity, institutional investor base, and public assets make a conventional foreign-currency funding crisis an inappropriate baseline. The loss is policy autonomy. More of the adjustment is imposed through yields, the exchange rate, regulated portfolios, taxation, and lower real returns rather than chosen through a planned fiscal strategy.

    10.6 Scenario C — External-shock acceleration¶

    An external shock is not a single scenario direction. Different shocks activate different boxes. Treating all of them as an accelerator toward the same endpoint would reproduce the deterministic error this chapter is designed to avoid.

    Shock First transmission Likely near-term relief Compression channel
    Energy or shipping shock Import prices, terms of trade, yen, firm cash flow Subsidies, reserve use, alternative procurement Higher borrowing, administered inflation, weaker real income
    Global recession Exports, profits, employment, tax revenue Lower global rates, possible yen appreciation, fiscal stimulus Larger primary deficit and delayed normalization
    Major domestic disaster Capital stock, logistics, local services, reconstruction Emergency budget, BOJ liquidity, accelerated grants Additional issuance and execution bottlenecks
    Sovereign rating action Risk perception, collateral rules, investor mandates Official communication and debt-management adjustment Higher term premium if the action validates existing fiscal concerns
    Global portfolio reallocation JGB futures, basis trades, yen funding, equities BOJ market operations and liquidity provision Amplified volatility and foreign-position unwinds

    An energy shock most directly strengthens the compression mechanism. Japan's FY2023 energy self-sufficiency rate was 15.3 percent, and fossil fuels still supplied roughly 70 percent of electricity generation. A sustained oil, LNG, or shipping shock weakens real income and firm margins at the same time that it lifts inflation. The BOJ must decide whether the shock is temporary, whether pass-through will become persistent, and whether tighter policy would worsen the real contraction. The government faces immediate pressure to restore or expand price relief.15

    The shock can move Scenario A toward Scenario B when the yen weakens with the terms-of-trade loss, subsidies enlarge the deficit, long yields rise on fiscal and inflation concerns, and the BOJ must choose between credibility and credit protection. It does not require reserve exhaustion. The relevant test is whether each round of relief and intervention produces less durable stabilization.

    A global recession is more ambiguous. Safe-haven flows and foreign rate cuts can strengthen the yen, lower imported inflation, and reduce JGB yields. Those effects temporarily widen the monetary corridor. At the same time, weaker exports, profits, wages, and tax revenue enlarge the primary deficit and create demand for another stimulus package. A recession can therefore postpone the currency-and-rate confrontation while worsening the fiscal stock inherited by the recovery. It is not automatically an acceleration of every box.

    A major disaster activates Japan's genuine fiscal strength. Rapid public spending, reconstruction finance, liquidity provision, and infrastructure coordination are precisely why fiscal space exists. The compressive effect depends on scale, timing, and what else is already being financed. A disaster becomes a timeline pull-forward when reconstruction arrives during a period of high marginal yields, recurring energy support, and limited construction labor. The constraint can be real capacity before it is money.

    A credit-rating action is a signal, not an independent cause. Japan's deep yen market and domestic regulation reduce the probability that a one-notch change mechanically prevents refinancing. Its effect would be larger if it formalized concerns already visible in auction demand, foreign portfolio rules, collateral haircuts, or the currency. The IMF assessed overall sovereign-stress risk as moderate because long maturities, the domestic investor base, reserve-currency status, and public assets mitigate the elevated debt level. A rating action must be read against those buffers.16

    A global portfolio unwind can accelerate the market clock without changing the fiscal data. Foreign investors hold a minority of outstanding JGBs but a much larger share of futures and secondary trading. Leveraged basis, relative-value, and currency positions can transmit stress quickly. The BOJ's financial-stability work warned that foreign non-bank and hedge-fund portfolio adjustment could amplify a decline in JGB liquidity.17

    Scenario C therefore functions as a branching node. The shock's first effect, the policy response, and the state of the buffers determine whether Japan returns to managed compression, crosses into market-enforced adjustment, or—through lower energy prices, global rate cuts, and stronger reform—moves temporarily toward stabilization.

    10.7 Scenario D — Successful stabilization¶

    Successful stabilization must be included as more than a ceremonial optimistic case. The slow-compression thesis is credible only if an alternative path can be described in observable terms.

    Stabilization does not require Japan to eliminate public debt, reverse population decline, or produce rapid real growth. It requires several constraints to ease together without shifting equivalent pressure into another box. The sequence matters.

    First, real wages must lead private demand. Nominal wage growth must remain above household inflation across large firms, SMEs, part-time work, and nonunion employment for several years. Consumption must continue rising after temporary transfers and price subsidies expire. The May 2026 wage improvement is an early condition for this path, not evidence that the transition is complete.18

    Second, productivity must finance the wage gain. Firms must pay higher wages through output, process redesign, pricing power grounded in value creation, and capital deepening rather than through lower margins, debt, or public subsidy. SME cost pass-through can improve without destabilizing inflation if productivity and competition absorb part of the increase. Business exit must move labor and assets to higher-productivity successors rather than remove local capacity.

    Third, fiscal improvement must survive the next budget cycle. A primary improvement caused by inflation, one-time fund income, or unused appropriations is not sufficient. Supplementary budgets must become smaller and restricted to genuinely unexpected shocks. Temporary tax measures need recurring offsets. Debt service must stabilize relative to revenue after higher-coupon debt has entered the stock. The IMF's recommended path—durable revenue and expenditure measures anchored by a medium-term framework, with supplementaries reserved for major shocks—describes the institutional direction even if the exact consolidation size is contested.19

    Fourth, BOJ normalization must proceed without forcing a return to disguised yield control. The policy rate can move toward the neutral range while private credit, housing, and banks remain functional. The yen must become less dependent on intervention, and market inflation expectations must remain anchored. Long yields can be higher; stabilization requires them to reflect ordinary nominal growth and duration risk rather than a rising fiscal premium.

    Fifth, private investors must replace part of the BOJ's marginal absorption. Banks, insurers, pensions, households, and overseas investors must accept JGB duration at yields consistent with sustainable fiscal arithmetic. Market-functioning surveys and liquidity measures should improve as BOJ holdings decline. A higher private share alone is not enough if it is achieved through regulatory coercion or very short issuance.

    Sixth, demographic adaptation must become capacity rather than participation arithmetic. Participation gains, foreign-worker retention, automation, care technology, regional service consolidation, and housing reform must raise delivered output per worker. Migration counts must translate into durable settlement, skills, and tax participation. Capital spending must move from authorization to completion.

    Seventh, energy policy must lower the next shock's fiscal cost. Nuclear restarts, grid investment, storage, renewable capacity, efficiency, and procurement diversification need not eliminate imports. They must reduce the frequency with which an external price move requires broad subsidies.

    This path is difficult because it requires coordination across institutions with different time horizons. Fiscal adjustment imposed before real wages and private demand strengthen can cause recession and generate another supplementary budget. BOJ tightening before firms and households can absorb it can weaken the fiscal plan. Productivity investment financed without project discipline can increase debt without raising capacity. Migration without local integration can enlarge administrative costs without solving retention.

    The successful sequence is therefore:

    broad real wage growth and productivity
    → stronger private consumption and tax revenue
    → withdrawal of generalized relief
    → credible multi-year fiscal improvement
    → BOJ normalization with stable private credit
    → durable JGB demand and a less intervention-dependent yen
    → lower shock sensitivity and a wider policy corridor.

    The Cabinet Office's growth cases show that such a path is mathematically possible. They also show its burden of proof. TFP must rise materially above the current-trend assumption, participation must continue increasing from an already high base, and primary surpluses must persist while interest and aging costs rise. Scenario D becomes the new base case only when those outcomes are observed together, not when they are appropriated in a strategy document.

    10.8 The warning-signal dashboard¶

    The dashboard is designed to prevent selective reading. Each indicator has a compression signal, a regime-transition signal, and a decompression signal. The categories are analytical; they are not official warning levels.

    Domain Compression signal Regime-transition signal Decompression signal
    Fiscal flow Debt service rises faster than recurring revenue; supplementaries remain routine High-rate debt-service path persists while new relief lacks recurring offsets Debt service stabilizes relative to revenue after refinancing; primary improvement survives one-offs
    JGB primary market Auctions clear with higher yields and variable concessions Repeated weak tails or demand across maturities; issuance shortened defensively Stable auction quality and private duration demand as BOJ purchases decline
    JGB secondary market Higher term premium; functioning improves only gradually Simultaneous liquidity deterioration, volatility, and renewed BOJ support Better survey functioning, trading depth, and price discovery with less BOJ ownership
    Monetary policy Gradual hikes with long pauses Rate increases coexist with larger bond purchases or repeated emergency operations Normalization proceeds without persistent market-function intervention or broad credit damage

    No row should be read in isolation. The dashboard's purpose is to identify direction across boxes. The May 2026 bond-market survey, for example, showed functioning still weak while the Bond Market Group reported improvement in liquidity and price discovery. Both can be true. A market emerging from heavy central-bank ownership can trade more freely and become more volatile at the same time.20

    The same discipline applies to bank data. Higher rates can improve net interest income before credit losses rise. The relevant signal is not a one-quarter profit increase or securities loss. It is whether the distribution of capital, credit cost, deposit funding, and lending behavior begins to split in a way that damages regional intermediation.

    10.9 Transition rules: when the scenario should change¶

    A practical framework needs a rule for changing its mind. The following rules are deliberately stricter than market commentary because they require persistence and cross-box confirmation.

    Transition from managed compression to market-enforced adjustment¶

    Scenario B should replace Scenario A as the working base case when at least four of the following six conditions persist for roughly two quarters or become unmistakably severe in a shorter period:

    1. Joint market rejection: the yen weakens while 10-year and super-long JGB yields rise, including after a BOJ hike or clearly hawkish repricing.
    2. Intervention decay: repeated official action produces successively shorter stabilization without a change in domestic or foreign policy expectations.
    3. Primary-market deterioration: several JGB auctions across maturities require abnormal concessions or produce weak demand relative to their own histories.
    4. Conflicted BOJ action: the Bank raises the policy rate or warns about inflation while simultaneously expanding long-bond support enough to blur the market-function distinction.
    5. Fiscal validation: a new tax cut, subsidy, disaster package, or recession response materially enlarges borrowing while debt service is already tracking toward the high-rate sensitivity.
    6. Credit transmission: corporate defaults, regional-bank credit costs, or lending retrenchment spread beyond firms already impaired before normalization.

    The two-quarter horizon is not a statistically estimated optimum. It is a safeguard against classifying an intraday currency move, one weak auction, or a global duration selloff as a domestic regime change. A severe event can override the waiting period, but the evidence must show transmission into policy or balance sheets.

    Transition from managed compression to successful stabilization¶

    Scenario D should replace Scenario A only after a broader and longer test. At least six of the following eight conditions should improve together for three fiscal years:

    1. broad real wages remain positive across employment categories;
    2. real private consumption expands after generalized relief expires;
    3. the structural primary balance improves after excluding one-off and inflation-related windfalls;
    4. supplementary budgets decline in size and are confined to unexpected shocks;
    5. debt service stabilizes relative to recurring revenue despite continued refinancing;
    6. BOJ normalization and purchase reduction proceed without destabilizing credit or the JGB market;
    7. the yen remains broadly stable without repeated intervention;
    8. realized productivity and labor-capacity gains offset a meaningful share of demographic drag.

    The three-year period is intentionally demanding. One year can be produced by oil prices, exchange rates, base effects, an election calendar, or a global boom. Stabilization requires the policy corridor to widen across a cycle.

    Transition from an external shock back to the structural path¶

    Scenario C is not a permanent destination. After the immediate shock, the analysis should ask where the residual liability sits. If the shock is absorbed through temporary relief that expires, private balance sheets recover, and the fiscal cost is contained, the system can return to Scenario A. If the response produces permanent expenditure, higher term premium, and conflicted BOJ action, it has migrated to Scenario B. If the shock catalyzes energy investment, fiscal reform, or labor reallocation that expands capacity, it can contribute to Scenario D.

    10.10 Falsification: what would disprove slow compression¶

    The monograph's thesis is not disproved because Japan avoids default. Avoiding conventional default is part of the thesis. Nor is it disproved by a stronger yen, a successful auction, a positive real-wage month, or a lower debt ratio. Those are relevant observations whose meaning depends on duration and interaction.

    The thesis is weakened when policy instruments regain independent effectiveness. That means the BOJ can normalize without forcing fiscal or credit rescue; fiscal policy can support a genuine shock without making temporary compensation structural; the yen can stabilize without repeated intervention; wages can raise demand without crushing SME margins; and demographic adaptation can increase capacity rather than merely slow decline.

    The following outcomes would provide serious contrary evidence:

    Debt service stabilizes as a share of recurring revenue after the low-coupon inheritance has materially repriced. A temporary improvement caused by large nominal tax gains is insufficient if the interest bill continues to catch up. The relevant observation begins after several years of higher marginal coupons.

    The primary balance improves structurally. The improvement must survive the treatment of supplementary budgets, special funds, temporary tax effects, and one-off asset income. A fiscal anchor must affect actual annual decisions rather than only the initial budget.

    Supplementary budgets cease to function as a second baseline. Emergency appropriations continue, but routine price relief, industrial programs, and known multi-year spending move into a transparent framework with offsets and exit rules. Both the OECD and IMF identified this institutional change as central to fiscal credibility.21

    Real wages and consumption rise broadly for several years. The gains must extend beyond unionized large firms and survive the end of energy subsidies and transfers. This would show that private income, rather than the state balance sheet, is carrying domestic demand.

    Private JGB demand strengthens as BOJ ownership and purchases decline. Auction quality, market-functioning surveys, and liquidity improve without regulated captive demand or a sharp shortening of issuance. Higher yields are compatible with this falsification if they reflect stable nominal growth rather than a rising risk premium.

    BOJ normalization proceeds beyond the hypothesized compression zone without destabilizing the system. A sustained policy rate above roughly 1.5 percent, accompanied by stable credit, manageable debt service, a functioning JGB market, and a less intervention-dependent yen, would weaken a central mechanism in Chapters 5 and 6. The outcome matters more than the label attached to the neutral rate.

    Productivity and labor adaptation offset demographic drag. Measured output per hour, firm-level investment returns, care productivity, foreign-worker retention, and regional service delivery improve. Authorized spending and participation targets alone do not meet the test.

    Energy support becomes genuinely temporary. The state can withdraw broad price subsidies because physical exposure, procurement risk, or household vulnerability has fallen—not because an adverse price shock happened to reverse for one season.

    No single item is sufficient because compression is a system claim. The precommitted rejection rule for the next full revision is therefore:

    If at least six of the eight decompression conditions in Section 10.9 are sustained for three fiscal years, and no equivalent liability has appeared in another box, managed compression should no longer be treated as the base case.

    If all eight improve across five years, the central thesis should be rejected rather than weakened. The correct conclusion would be that Japan used its remaining buffers to construct a new stability regime before markets imposed adjustment.

    10.11 The forecast and update discipline¶

    The evidence cutoff of July 31, 2026 must remain fixed for this edition. Later facts should enter an update ledger and then a revised edition. Quietly inserting post-cutoff evidence would make the scenario claims impossible to evaluate.

    Each update should record six fields:

    Field Required entry
    Date and release When the evidence became available, not only the period it covers
    Variable and boundary Exact fiscal, market, population, or survey definition
    Prior expectation What the monograph expected before the release
    Observed result The measured fact without scenario interpretation
    Scenario effect Strengthens A, B, C, or D; weakens the thesis; or remains ambiguous
    Assumption change Whether a monitoring band, mechanism, or scenario ranking must change

    The forecast log should preserve several current hypotheses in explicit form.

    1. Managed compression is the base case. Japan is expected to remain financeable and institutionally stable while fiscal and distributional costs rise.
    2. The first visible inflection is most likely during 2027–2030. The reason is refinancing and policy collision, not a demographic cliff.
    3. Approximately 50 trillion yen of debt service is a stress path. It must be withdrawn if rates or spending fall sufficiently below the sensitivity assumptions.
    4. The 1.25–1.5 percent policy zone is a practical hypothesis. It must be revised upward if credit, banks, budgets, housing, and the yen absorb higher rates together.
    5. USD/JPY 165 and 170 are reaction zones, not crash levels. They lose value as monitoring bands if global rates or official reaction functions change.
    6. Intervention decay is conditional. Coordinated intervention paired with a credible policy shift can become durable and would weaken the claim that the yen remains the principal pressure valve.
    7. Demography is a multiplier. This claim must be revised if productivity, migration retention, care technology, and regional consolidation produce observable capacity faster than the workforce contracts.

    This discipline prevents a moving-target thesis. If the yen stabilizes, the analysis cannot redefine weakness as any rate that seems inconvenient. If debt service remains well below the stress path, the 50-trillion-yen marker cannot be preserved as an indefinitely delayed prediction. If broad real wages produce private demand, the household mechanism must be reduced in weight.

    10.12 Counterarguments and limitations¶

    Scenario analysis cannot eliminate uncertainty. Five limitations are especially important.

    The scenarios are not mutually exclusive. External shock is a transition mechanism, and successful stabilization can develop inside managed compression. The categories organize causality; they do not divide history into clean boxes.

    Global variables can dominate Japanese policy for long periods. A rapid Federal Reserve easing cycle, lower oil prices, or global risk aversion could strengthen the yen and lower JGB yields without domestic reform. That outcome would widen the near-term corridor and should be recognized. It would not by itself establish a durable fiscal or demographic solution.

    Policy changes the forecast. If authorities respond to these warning signals with credible reform, the predicted pressure may not occur. That is not a failure of analysis if the update ledger records the intervention and the mechanism through which risk declined. It is a failure only if any outcome is retroactively claimed as confirmation.

    Political tolerances are not directly observable. The practical compression zone and reaction bands are inferred from policy behavior, market pricing, and distributional pressure. An election, leadership change, or social shift can alter tolerance quickly. Numerical bands must therefore remain conditional.

    Official and private data measure different universes. Debt service includes redemption transfers unless otherwise specified; interest expenditure does not. JGB ownership can be reported at market or face value and with different instrument boundaries. Wage surveys exclude parts of the labor market. Bankruptcy databases apply liability thresholds and case classifications. Scenario signals must preserve those definitions.

    There is also a deeper counterargument: Japan may continue transferring pressure indefinitely because the transfers occur inside a consolidated national balance sheet. The government borrows from institutions funded by domestic saving; the BOJ remits income to the state; households hold deposits backed partly by public securities; foreign assets generate income; and inflation reduces the real value of inherited liabilities.

    This argument explains why Scenario A can persist much longer than a conventional crisis framework suggests. It does not make the transfers neutral. Households, banks, firms, the BOJ, and the government are not one agent with one time horizon and one welfare function. Inflation redistributes between savers and borrowers. Higher coupons redistribute toward asset holders and away from future budgets. Intervention exchanges a foreign asset for yen. A subsidy protects the current household while creating a future public claim. Consolidation removes double counting; it does not remove distribution, timing, or political constraint.

    The final limitation is that successful stabilization may be easier than the scenario suggests if several moderate improvements reinforce one another. Japan may not need TFP at 1.4 percent, participation at 68.3 percent, and a large primary surplus simultaneously. Lower global rates, a stronger yen, nuclear restarts, broader wage growth, better targeted transfers, and modest entitlement reform could widen the corridor without matching the Cabinet Office's strongest case. Scenario D is demanding because the current pressures are joint, not because one heroic growth assumption is indispensable.

    10.13 Finding: the test is whether the corridor widens¶

    The scenarios produce a direct answer to the monograph's forward question.

    Japan's most likely path is not a sudden sovereign rupture. It is managed compression: gradual BOJ normalization, higher refinancing costs, a weak and intervention-sensitive yen, recurring fiscal compensation, selective firm and regional stress, and continued institutional stability. The state retains enough capacity to prevent most individual pressures from becoming an acute national event.

    Market-enforced adjustment becomes the operative alternative when prices and portfolio demand begin to dictate policy sequencing. The decisive signature is not a number on one screen. It is a cluster: long yields and the yen move against the desired policy mix, intervention decays, auctions require repeated concessions, the BOJ uses conflicting instruments, fiscal compensation expands, and credit stress spreads beyond the already weak tail.

    External shocks can accelerate that transition, but they do not all point in the same direction. Energy and shipping shocks intensify the currency-inflation-fiscal loop. Recession can relieve rates and the yen while damaging revenue and primary balances. Disaster tests real execution capacity. Rating action matters most when it confirms a repricing already underway.

    Successful stabilization remains possible. It requires broad real wages, productivity, private demand, fiscal institutional reform, durable JGB absorption, orderly BOJ normalization, energy investment, and demographic adaptation to reinforce one another. Japan does not need to become young, debt-free, or high-growth. It needs to stop solving each constraint by enlarging another.

    The falsification test follows from that distinction. Slow compression is rejected when the feasible policy set expands across several boxes for several years and no equivalent liability is merely hidden elsewhere. Until that occurs, nominal stability alone is not evidence of restored sovereignty. It is evidence that the transfer system continues to work.

    The conclusion draws the argument together. Japan remains capable of financing itself and managing shocks, but that capability increasingly depends on transferring adjustment to households, the currency, future budgets, SMEs, regional institutions, and the BOJ's balance sheet. The central risk is not immediate insolvency. It is the gradual loss of the ability to use policy instruments independently.
