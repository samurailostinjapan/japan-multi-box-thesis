    Chapter 11 — Conclusion: The Erosion of Policy Sovereignty¶

    Japan's central economic problem is not an inability to finance itself. It is the declining ability to preserve every major policy objective at the same time.

    The distinction is decisive. A conventional sovereign-crisis framework asks whether the government can issue debt, service its obligations, or defend the banking system. Japan retains exceptional capacity in all three areas. Its liabilities are overwhelmingly denominated in yen. The domestic financial system remains large and liquid. The Bank of Japan can supply reserves and purchase government securities. The Ministry of Finance can alter the maturity and composition of issuance, draw on a substantial foreign-reserve portfolio, and intervene in the currency market. Households and corporations hold large financial assets, while the country maintains an exceptionally strong external balance sheet. None of these institutions was close to exhaustion at the evidence cutoff of July 31, 2026.

    The more difficult question is what happens when those stabilizers no longer reinforce one another. A low policy rate protects the budget and fragile borrowers but leaves the yen exposed. A higher rate strengthens the inflation signal but accelerates public and private refinancing. Bond purchases contain yields but weaken market price discovery and can dilute the currency effect of tightening. Fiscal relief supports household demand but converts import-price pressure into borrowing or lost revenue. Yen depreciation improves the domestic value of overseas income and assets while raising the cost of food, energy, and industrial inputs. Demographic contraction increases the urgency of productivity investment while reducing the labor, fiscal, and administrative capacity available to execute it.

    The argument of this monograph is that these are no longer separate tradeoffs. They form a tightening system in which the side effect of one instrument increasingly enters the decision rule of another. Japan can still act. What it is losing is the ability to act in one domain without paying an immediate price in another.

    That is the erosion of policy sovereignty.

    11.1 The answer to the research question¶

    The principal research question asked how Japan's fiscal-dominance regime is transforming a system of policy-controlled stability into one of market-driven constraint, and why policies that relieve one pressure increasingly intensify another.

    The evidence supports a five-part answer.

    First, the old stability regime depended on an unusual alignment of low inflation, low yields, domestic financial surplus, BOJ absorption, stable institutional demand, and political tolerance for incremental adjustment. Those conditions gave the state time. Deflation and weak nominal demand were economically damaging, but they kept financing costs low. Corporate caution constrained investment, but it generated financial surplus. Demographic aging reduced potential growth, but it also lowered the natural rate and supported demand for safe assets. The weaknesses of one part of the system became stabilizers for another.

    Second, the return of inflation broke that alignment before the fiscal system had been repaired. The BOJ could no longer suppress yields and expand its balance sheet without considering the yen, import prices, and market functioning. Yet normalization began against a debt stock accumulated under near-zero rates and an economy in which many firms, households, and public institutions had adapted to negligible borrowing costs. The transition therefore repriced the legacy of the old regime rather than beginning from a clean balance sheet.

    Third, refinancing transmits that repricing gradually. Japan does not reprice its entire debt stock at the marginal yield. Low-coupon securities mature over time and are replaced at prevailing rates. This lag is a stabilizer because it prevents an immediate fiscal shock. It is also the mechanism through which higher interest costs become embedded across successive budgets. The absence of an abrupt break does not mean the constraint has failed to arrive. It means the constraint is entering as a flow.

    Fourth, the cost of preserving aggregate stability is allocated unevenly. Households absorb import inflation and restrain discretionary consumption. Large international firms use foreign earnings, pricing power, and cash reserves. Smaller firms absorb incomplete cost pass-through through margins and borrowing. Banks receive some benefit from higher asset yields before weaker borrowers and low-coupon securities create losses. The state compensates visible constituencies through subsidies, transfers, tax reductions, and supplementary budgets. National aggregates can therefore remain stable while the institutions supporting domestic demand become less resilient.

    Fifth, markets increasingly determine the price at which these transfers can continue. The government retains legal authority over taxation, spending, issuance, and regulation. The BOJ retains control over the overnight policy rate and substantial influence over the JGB market. The Ministry of Finance retains the capacity to intervene in foreign exchange. Market-driven constraint does not mean that private investors seize formal control. It means that yields, the exchange rate, auction behavior, inflation expectations, and credit conditions increasingly determine the sequence and cost of public decisions.

    The transition can be stated compactly:

    the old regime suppressed several prices together
    → inflation made that suppression harder to maintain
    → normalization began to reprice inherited public and private liabilities
    → fiscal and political compensation absorbed the most visible losses
    → compensation increased the future cost of normalization
    → markets gained greater influence over the feasible combination of rates, spending, and currency policy.

    Japan's fiscal dominance is therefore not a claim that the BOJ takes direct orders from the government. It is a claim about the scale of the side effects. Monetary decisions operate on a fiscal base large enough that debt service, bank balance sheets, SME credit, housing, and political compensation constrain the range of practicable action. Legal independence survives. Economic separability weakens.

    11.2 What the monograph establishes—and what it does not¶

    The chapters support several findings more precise than the proposition that Japan has too much debt or too little growth.

    Question Finding Claim rejected
    What is the immediate fiscal mechanism? Higher marginal yields enter the budget through gradual refinancing and recurrent primary additions The entire debt stock reprices at once
    What constrains the BOJ? Tightening remains possible, but its fiscal, credit, housing, and banking effects increasingly shape the path The BOJ is technically unable to raise rates
    What role does the yen play? It absorbs pressure created by restrained domestic rates, fiscal signaling, and external-price exposure Depreciation is either purely beneficial or purely destructive
    Who bears the adjustment? Households, SMEs, borrowers, banks, the currency, and future budgets carry different portions at different times Aggregate corporate profit or household wealth describes the whole economy
    What does demography do? It reduces the future number of balance sheets, workers, institutions, and taxpayers available to absorb shocks Population decline assigns an immediate sovereign-crisis date
    Why do temporary measures matter? Recurring compensation changes the realized fiscal stance and political baseline Every subsidy is wasteful or every supplement is exceptional
    When does constraint become market driven? When prices and portfolio demand dictate sequencing even though formal instruments remain available A failed auction or default is required before markets matter

    These findings do not establish imminent insolvency. They do not imply that Japan must experience a banking crisis, uncontrolled inflation, or a currency collapse. They do not prove that the 1.25–1.5 percent policy range is an official ceiling, that debt service must reach 50 trillion yen, or that USD/JPY must follow a predetermined path. Those figures are monitoring zones and conditional stress results, not terminal forecasts.

    The argument is narrower and more consequential. Japan's institutions can prevent rupture for a long time because they are designed to absorb and redistribute pressure. The same capacity can obscure the cumulative cost. Each successful intervention becomes evidence both of resilience and of the need to deploy another balance sheet. The relevant question is not whether the intervention worked on the day it was used. It is whether the underlying exposure fell, whether the instrument can be withdrawn, and whether another institution inherited the liability.

    That standard explains why nominal stability is insufficient as a verdict. A falling debt ratio can coexist with a rising debt-service burden. Positive real wages can coexist with weak household consumption and incomplete coverage across the labor market. Higher bank earnings can coexist with growing risk among vulnerable borrowers. A stronger yen after intervention can coexist with an unchanged rate and fiscal configuration. A supplementary budget can protect households and fund necessary reconstruction while also weakening the distinction between emergency spending and the ordinary fiscal baseline.

    The system must be evaluated through joint movement, not isolated improvements.

    11.3 Financing capacity remains formidable¶

    The strongest objection to the compression thesis is also one of its premises: Japan possesses buffers that most high-debt states do not.

    The government borrows in its own currency and is not exposed to a conventional foreign-currency funding stop. The weighted-average maturity of the debt delays the transmission of current yields. Domestic banks, insurers, pension funds, and other institutions continue to hold large quantities of JGBs. The BOJ remains the dominant single holder and can supply unlimited yen liquidity against eligible collateral. Japan's net international investment position and reserve assets reduce external-solvency risk. The banking system remained capitalized in the BOJ's April 2026 assessment, and household and corporate financial assets provide substantial private shock absorption.1

    These are not accounting curiosities. They explain why repeated warnings based solely on the gross-debt ratio failed. A state with yen liabilities, a long maturity profile, a domestic financial base, a central bank capable of market intervention, and large external assets does not behave like a sovereign dependent on short-term foreign-currency creditors.

    The error begins when national capacity is treated as costless fiscal capacity. The government's debt and the BOJ's holdings can be consolidated for some analytical purposes, but households, banks, corporations, the central bank, and the general account do not share one budget constraint or one political objective. Interest paid on JGBs becomes income to asset holders and a charge to future budgets. Inflation reduces the real value of public liabilities while reducing the purchasing power of cash, deposits, wages, and pensions. Central-bank purchases change the maturity and interest sensitivity of the consolidated public balance sheet, but they also affect market functioning, bank reserves, remittances, and the exchange-rate signal.

    The same limit applies to external wealth. Japan's national foreign assets generate income and support confidence. Most are not liquid resources owned by the general account. A corporate foreign subsidiary, a pension fund's overseas portfolio, and an official reserve security all strengthen the national balance sheet in different ways. They cannot be substituted one-for-one for tax revenue or ordinary budget financing.

    Japan's buffers therefore change the form and timing of adjustment. They make default less likely, buy time for policy, and allow losses to be spread across institutions. They do not remove the real resource cost of energy imports, care labor, debt service, disaster reconstruction, defense, or an aging population. Nor do they guarantee that the distribution produced by the transfer system remains politically acceptable.

    The correct conclusion is not that Japan has exhausted its buffers. It is that the buffers increasingly have competing uses.

    11.4 Refinancing is the fiscal clock¶

    The gross-debt stock defines scale, but refinancing defines time.

    FY2026 national debt service was budgeted at 31.3 trillion yen. In its July 2026 sensitivity analysis, the Ministry of Finance projected debt service of 41.3 trillion yen in FY2029 under its 3-percent nominal-growth case and 40.3 trillion yen under its 1.5-percent case. When interest rates ran two percentage points above the first baseline from FY2027, debt service reached 48.9 trillion yen in FY2029.2 The monograph's approximately 50-trillion-yen result is drawn from that published sensitivity. It is not the ministry's baseline and should be withdrawn if the rate and expenditure assumptions fail to materialize.

    The result matters because debt service competes with current policy even when the debt ratio is stable or declining. Nominal growth can improve tax receipts and reduce the ratio's denominator. Inflation can raise nominal revenue before the full debt stock reprices. Both effects can produce a favorable interval in which the debt ratio falls while the future interest burden is accumulating. The interval is useful, but it is not self-validating. If nominal revenue gains are committed to permanent spending, broad tax reductions, or recurring relief before refinancing costs arrive, the apparent dividend has already been spent.

    This is why the realized fiscal stance cannot be read from the initial budget alone. Supplementary budgets are legitimate instruments for disasters, recessions, security shocks, and urgent relief. Their regular use, debt financing, and inclusion of multi-year programs make them part of the empirical baseline. From FY2016 through FY2023, bonds supplied 74.5 percent of the financing resources in the Board of Audit's review of supplementary budgets. The confirmed FY2026 first supplement added 3.1135 trillion yen and financed the addition entirely with special deficit-financing bonds.3

    The fiscal constraint is therefore neither a fixed debt ceiling nor an approaching day when buyers disappear. It is the increasing share of annual political capacity absorbed before new policy begins. Debt service, social security, defense, reconstruction, energy relief, family policy, and regional support are not equivalent claims, but they draw from the same future revenue and issuance capacity.

    Long maturities make the process slow. Recurrent primary additions make it cumulative. That combination is why the first visible inflection is more likely to appear as budget compression during 2027–2030 than as a discrete solvency event.

    11.5 Monetary normalization exposes the collision¶

    The BOJ's normalization is both necessary to the new regime and constrained by the legacy of the old one.

    By July 2026, the policy rate stood at 1 percent. The Bank judged financial conditions accommodative, maintained that the financial system was broadly stable, and warned that underlying inflation could exceed 2 percent. Published estimates placed the nominal neutral rate across a wide range rather than at a single observable point.4 The monograph's 1.25–1.5 percent compression zone is therefore not a claim about the BOJ's technical limit. It is a hypothesis about the point at which the side effects of further tightening may become politically and financially binding.

    The Bank can move above that range. If broad wages, credit quality, bank earnings, housing, the budget, and the yen absorb the increase together, the hypothesis should be revised upward. Conversely, the constraint could appear below it if higher long yields, fiscal expansion, or an external shock tighten conditions independently of the overnight rate.

    The policy collision is asymmetric. Holding rates low supports borrowers and delays fiscal repricing, but it preserves a rate differential and a negative-real-rate environment that can weaken the yen. Depreciation raises import costs and household pressure, encouraging fiscal compensation. Raising rates improves inflation credibility and may support the currency, but it also increases marginal public borrowing costs and passes into corporate, mortgage, and bank balance sheets. A small increase can therefore be large enough to matter domestically and too small to alter the exchange-rate regime.

    JGB purchases do not eliminate the collision. After formally ending yield-curve control, the BOJ continued to manage the pace of purchases and retained the ability to respond to disorderly conditions. That capacity protects market functioning. Used aggressively, it can also obscure the fiscal signal conveyed by long yields and weaken the consistency of a tightening message.5

    Foreign-exchange intervention has the same limitation. It can impose large losses on speculative positions, interrupt one-way trading, and change the short-term path of the currency. It cannot independently remove an interest differential, an inflation differential, an energy-import requirement, or a fiscal signal. The officially confirmed April–May 2026 intervention demonstrated substantial firepower, while the July episode showed how quickly currency and bond markets could reach different judgments about the same anticipated policy path.6

    The BOJ retains monetary control in the narrow operational sense. What has narrowed is the number of outcomes that one monetary action can improve simultaneously.

    11.6 Distribution is the adjustment mechanism¶

    Macro stability is produced through microeconomic absorption.

    Weak-yen inflation does not enter every balance sheet in the same way. Exporters and firms with overseas assets can receive translation gains. Import-intensive businesses, households, utilities, transport operators, food processors, and smaller suppliers face higher costs. Large firms can hedge, reprice, diversify financing, or shift costs through supply chains. SMEs often have fewer options and absorb the difference through margin, borrowing, labor adjustment, or exit.

    The wage data at the cutoff prevented a simple deterioration narrative. Real cash earnings had turned positive, and the 2026 spring wage round produced substantial settlements. Those developments were genuine evidence against a permanent real-wage decline. The open question was breadth: whether gains reached workers outside large unionized firms, whether they exceeded food and energy inflation for long enough to restore purchasing power, and whether SMEs financed them through productivity rather than margin compression or higher prices.7

    The same discipline applies to banks. Higher rates can improve net interest income and restore a more normal lending business. The system was not approaching generalized failure at the cutoff. Yet normalization distributes gains and losses unevenly across securities portfolios, deposit franchises, loan books, regions, and borrower quality. Credit stress can remain concentrated for years before it becomes systemic. That is precisely why failures in the vulnerable tail matter without proving a national banking crisis.8

    Households are the residual shock absorber when wages, transfers, and prices do not align. They reduce discretionary consumption, draw on savings, alter housing choices, extend loan duration, or accept a lower real value of fixed income. Pension indexation and household financial wealth offer partial protection, but exposure varies by age, income, asset composition, housing tenure, and consumption basket.

    Political compensation closes the loop. Subsidies and transfers can be justified on distributional and macroeconomic grounds, especially during an extreme shock. They prevent a rapid fall in demand and protect households unable to absorb volatile energy or food prices. The compression mechanism begins when recurring compensation substitutes for a reduction in the underlying exposure. The private loss becomes a public liability, and the public liability raises the future cost of the monetary and fiscal regime.

    This is not a claim that the burden always moves downward or that every large firm benefits. It is a claim that national aggregates conceal bargaining power. The institutions able to reprice tend to preserve their balance sheets. Those unable to reprice absorb the shock or appeal to the state. The distribution of pricing power is therefore one of the principal mechanisms through which slow compression becomes politically durable.

    11.7 Demography removes the margin for repeated deferral¶

    Demography does not supply the first market trigger. It determines the capacity available after the trigger.

    The near-term 2027–2030 inflection identified in this monograph arises from refinancing, BOJ normalization, yen pressure, fiscal additions, and political response. Population decline does not suddenly become relevant in a single year. Its influence is slower and less reversible. The workers available in the 2030s are largely drawn from cohorts already alive. Improvements in fertility are essential to the later-century path but cannot materially expand the labor force during the first refinancing horizon.

    Japan has adapted more successfully than crude demographic arithmetic suggests. Women's and older-worker participation rose. Foreign employment expanded. Firms invested in automation. A smaller labor force increased scarcity and created bargaining power for some workers. Older households retained large stocks of financial assets. These responses weakened the relationship between population decline and immediate economic contraction.

    They did not eliminate the capacity problem. Participation cannot rise without limit. Automation replaces tasks rather than taxpayers, caregivers, customers, or local institutions in equal proportions. Foreign labor can slow workforce decline, but the fiscal and social benefit depends on retention, wage progression, family formation, housing, language access, and institutional integration. Regional depopulation lowers demand while leaving roads, water systems, hospitals, schools, transport networks, and public administration with high fixed costs. Metropolitan areas face a different problem: not disappearance of settlements, but large absolute demand for health and care services.9

    Demography therefore changes every proposed solution. Entitlement reform becomes politically harder as the beneficiary share grows. Productivity investment becomes more urgent while execution capacity shrinks. Migration becomes more necessary while integration costs increase. Regional consolidation saves resources but imposes concentrated local losses. Higher wages support demand but increase care, service, and SME costs unless productivity follows.

    The old regime could defer adjustment because time itself was cheap. Low yields reduced the fiscal cost of delay, rising participation added workers, and domestic saving supported intermediation. The new regime faces positive refinancing costs and a smaller margin of labor and institutional redundancy. Deferral remains possible. It no longer arrives at a negligible price.

    11.8 Political management preserves legitimacy and narrows the corridor¶

    The subsidy state is the political form of stress transfer.

    Japanese governments did not create recurring supplementary budgets, energy support, tax relief, and targeted transfers because policymakers failed to understand fiscal arithmetic. These instruments respond to a real political problem: the benefits of weak-yen inflation, monetary normalization, or structural reform are dispersed and uncertain, while the losses are immediate and concentrated. A household sees the utility bill. An SME sees the refinancing notice. A region sees the hospital or transport operator at risk. The future interest burden remains abstract.

    Compensation can therefore be rational in each episode and compressive across episodes. It smooths shocks, protects demand, and prevents concentrated losses from breaking political consent. It also raises the baseline from which the next shock must be financed. Measures described as temporary become difficult to withdraw if the underlying exposure—energy dependence, weak real income, regional fragility, or currency pressure—persists.

    The distinction is not between spending and austerity. It is between spending that creates future capacity and spending that repeatedly purchases time. Grid investment, energy diversification, care technology, child support, migration infrastructure, disaster resilience, and productivity investment can widen the future corridor if they are executed effectively. Broad price suppression and unfunded permanent tax reductions may protect the present while doing less to reduce the next claim on the budget.

    The Takaichi administration matters in this framework as an accelerator, not an origin. Its fiscal commitments, relief measures, food-tax announcement, and confrontation with a normalizing BOJ made existing contradictions easier to observe and price.10 The underlying regime was built across decades. Political acceleration changed the speed at which markets incorporated its implications.

    This produces the central political collision:

    visible adjustment is compensated
    → compensation preserves near-term legitimacy
    → the fiscal baseline and refinancing burden rise
    → future monetary and fiscal choices become more costly
    → the next visible adjustment requires further compensation.

    The loop does not continue because the state lacks power. It continues because the state has enough power to defer the distributional settlement.

    11.9 Policy sovereignty is a question of effective choice¶

    Sovereignty is often treated as binary: a country either controls its currency and institutions or it does not. That definition is legally important and economically incomplete.

    This monograph distinguishes three levels.

    Level Meaning Japan at the evidence cutoff
    Legal authority Formal power to tax, spend, issue yen debt, set monetary policy, regulate finance, and intervene in FX Intact
    Instrument capacity Operational ability to use budgets, JGB issuance, BOJ liquidity, bond purchases, reserves, and regulation Substantial
    Effective policy autonomy Ability to use one instrument without another constraint dictating the timing, scale, or reversal Narrowing

    The third level is the object of the monograph. Policy sovereignty erodes when technically available instruments become mutually conditional. The BOJ can raise rates, but the refinancing path and credit system determine how far and how fast. The government can expand relief, but JGB pricing and future debt service determine the cost. MOF can intervene, but the monetary-fiscal mix determines whether appreciation persists. Banks can absorb JGBs, but capital, deposits, duration exposure, and regional credit demand determine the yield required. Households can dissave, but age, income, and confidence determine how long private assets can substitute for real income.

    Markets gain influence in this environment without issuing commands. A successful auction can still contain evidence of constraint if it requires a persistent yield concession. A currency intervention can still succeed tactically while revealing that the exchange rate is resisting the domestic policy mix. A BOJ purchase operation can restore liquidity while confirming that private absorption is inadequate at the desired price. The market contribution is not veto. It is price discovery.

    This definition also explains why Japan can remain stable for years after policy autonomy begins to narrow. Legal authority and instrument capacity are durable. They allow officials to manage volatility, distribute losses, change maturities, adjust regulation, mobilize reserves, and buy time. Effective autonomy declines earlier because each choice arrives with a smaller set of acceptable companions.

    The shift from policy-controlled stability to market-driven constraint is therefore not an event. It is a change in who determines the marginal terms of continuity.

    11.10 Stabilization requires capacity, not a miracle¶

    Japan does not need to eliminate its public debt, reverse population aging, or produce permanently high real growth to widen the corridor. It needs several moderate improvements to reinforce one another rather than collide.

    The fiscal requirement is not immediate balance at any economic cost. It is a credible distinction between temporary shocks and permanent commitments, combined with enough primary improvement to prevent higher interest costs from consuming revenue gains. The monetary requirement is not a mechanically high policy rate. It is a normalization path that stabilizes inflation expectations and the yen without requiring recurrent suppression of the long end or generating a broad credit contraction. The household requirement is not one year of large-firm wage settlements. It is broad real-income growth capable of sustaining consumption after subsidies expire.

    The structural requirement is similarly concrete. Productivity gains must appear in measured output, service capacity, and SME margins rather than only in investment appropriations. Migration must produce durable participation and settlement rather than a rotating supply of temporary labor. Energy policy must reduce the economy's physical exposure to imported price shocks rather than only change who pays the bill. Regional reform must maintain essential services with fewer workers and users. Financial institutions must absorb a larger share of JGB issuance and credit repricing without depending on renewed permanent BOJ dominance.

    No single improvement is enough. Faster nominal growth helps the debt denominator but can damage households if it is driven by import inflation. A stronger yen helps purchasing power but can reflect a global recession that weakens revenue and corporate earnings. Higher wages help households but can accelerate prices or SME failures if productivity and pass-through do not follow. Fiscal consolidation helps credibility but can reduce demand if imposed abruptly. The objective is compatibility.

    Successful stabilization would therefore look less dramatic than either crisis or miracle. Supplementary budgets would become smaller and more genuinely exceptional. Debt service would stabilize relative to revenue after temporary inflation effects faded. Real wages and consumption would improve across the labor market. Private JGB demand and market functioning would strengthen as BOJ support declined. Energy relief could be withdrawn because exposure had fallen. Productivity and migration would preserve real service capacity despite population decline. Several boxes would improve at the same time, and no equivalent liability would appear elsewhere.

    That outcome is possible. The evidence cutoff did not show that it had been achieved.

    11.11 Uncertainty, falsification, and the next test¶

    The conclusion remains conditional on a fixed July 31, 2026 evidence base. Later data can strengthen, weaken, or overturn it. They should enter through the update discipline established in Chapter 10 rather than being inserted retrospectively into this edition.

    Several developments could widen Japan's policy corridor without immediate domestic reform. Lower global interest rates could reduce the international differential and support the yen. Lower energy prices could weaken imported inflation and reduce the need for subsidies. A global risk-off episode could increase demand for yen and JGBs. These changes would matter. They could provide years of additional room. Their durability would depend on whether the time was converted into fiscal and structural capacity.

    The thesis is weakened if broad real wages, private consumption, the primary balance, JGB demand, market functioning, energy exposure, and policy normalization improve together. It is materially weakened if the BOJ moves beyond the hypothesized compression zone while the yen stabilizes, credit remains sound, debt service stays manageable, and fiscal compensation declines. It should be rejected if the feasible policy set expands across most of the eight boxes for several years without an equivalent liability being shifted into another sector.11

    The opposite discipline also applies. A single weak auction, currency intervention, bankruptcy, natural disaster, or month of falling real wages does not prove the thesis. Compression is a system claim. Its empirical signature is the repeated coupling of markets, budgets, household relief, credit, and institutional response. Confirmation requires persistence and joint movement.

    The most important uncertainties are political. Economic models can estimate debt-service sensitivity, population paths, or bank stress under specified assumptions. They cannot identify in advance how much inflation households will tolerate, how rapidly the government will compensate losses, whether a temporary tax reduction will be restored, or when investors will demand a larger fiscal-risk premium. Political tolerance determines the boundaries of the feasible set as much as accounting does.

    This uncertainty is not a reason to abandon the framework. It is the reason to monitor the corridor rather than predict a crisis date.

    11.12 Final finding: stability no longer proves control¶

    Japan remains financially capable of avoiding a conventional sovereign crisis. It can issue yen debt, mobilize domestic institutions, use the BOJ's balance sheet, draw on reserves, manage maturities, regulate the financial system, and compensate households. Its accumulated public, private, and external assets make abrupt discontinuity less likely than the gross-debt headline implies.

    That capacity does not make adjustment costless. It changes where the cost appears.

    When rates are restrained, the yen and imported prices absorb pressure. When rates rise, debt service, borrowers, banks, and housing absorb it. When households are protected, the budget absorbs it. When the budget is protected through inflation, savers and fixed incomes absorb it. When firms preserve margins, workers, suppliers, or consumers absorb it. When reform is delayed, the remaining workforce and future budgets inherit it.

    Japan's stabilizers still work. The problem is that they increasingly work by transferring pressure rather than removing exposure. Each transfer can be justified, financed, and administered. Their accumulation narrows the number of policy combinations that remain economically and politically tolerable.

    The central risk is therefore not that Japan suddenly loses the technical ability to meet its obligations. It is that policy becomes progressively reactive: the BOJ responds to the yen while watching the budget; the government responds to prices while watching JGBs; banks respond to higher yields while watching borrowers; households respond to wages while watching food and energy; and markets respond to every proposed solution by pricing the constraint it enlarges.

    This is slow compression. It is a regime in which nominal continuity survives while independent policy effectiveness declines.

    The endpoint is not predetermined. Japan can use the long refinancing lag, institutional wealth, labor adaptation, technological capacity, and political stability to build a new equilibrium. But the standard for success is higher than continued financing, a temporary currency rebound, or a falling debt ratio. The policy corridor must widen. Several constraints must ease together. Temporary relief must become durable capacity.

    Until then, stability should not be mistaken for sovereignty. Japan can still choose. It is the range of choices—and the freedom to make one without another institution paying the price—that is being lost.
