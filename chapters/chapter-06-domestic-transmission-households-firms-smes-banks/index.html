    Chapter 6 — Domestic Transmission: Households, Firms, SMEs, and Banks¶

    The monetary and fiscal constraints examined in Chapters 4 and 5 do not enter the domestic economy as a uniform national loss. They alter relative prices, bargaining power, financing costs, asset income, and the timing of adjustment. A weak yen raises the yen value of foreign earnings while increasing the cost of imported food, fuel, materials, and equipment. Higher interest rates improve income on newly priced financial assets while raising the cost of loans and exposing losses on older securities. Wage growth supports household income but becomes an additional cost for firms that cannot raise productivity or selling prices. Fiscal transfers protect consumption but move part of the adjustment onto the state’s balance sheet.

    The central question is therefore distributional: who absorbs the cost of maintaining nominal stability when the available policy instruments have conflicting domestic effects?

    The answer cannot be derived from a single aggregate. Japan can report record corporate profits while import-dependent firms lose margin. Average real wages can rise while part-time workers, pensioners, and households with food-heavy budgets experience weaker purchasing power. Banks can earn more from wider interest margins while selected regional lenders face concentrated credit losses and duration risk. Business sentiment can remain favorable even as bankruptcies rise among the smallest firms.

    This chapter does not argue that Japan’s domestic economy was in generalized distress by July 2026. The evidence rejects that claim. Employment remained tight, financial conditions were still accommodative, nominal wages were rising, corporate profits remained high in aggregate, and the Bank of Japan judged the banking system resilient to severe stress scenarios.1 The argument is narrower: the mechanisms preserving aggregate stability distribute pressure unevenly, and the most exposed groups possess the least capacity to absorb repeated shocks.

    That uneven transmission is the domestic core of slow compression. The regime remains stable partly because households reduce discretionary consumption, firms accept lower margins, borrowers extend maturities, banks reprice credit gradually, and the government compensates selected losses. None of these adjustments is individually catastrophic. Together they weaken the conversion of nominal income into broad real demand.

    6.1 The adjustment is allocated through five balance sheets¶

    The domestic transmission mechanism can be organized around five balance sheets: households, large firms, small and medium-sized enterprises, banks, and the state. Each receives a different combination of gains and losses.

    Sector Potential gain Principal loss Typical adjustment
    Households Higher nominal wages, deposit income, equity wealth Food and energy inflation, mortgage repricing, weaker real income Reduce discretionary consumption or draw on savings
    Large firms Foreign-earnings translation, pricing power, capital-market access Imported inputs, tariffs, wage costs, weaker domestic demand Reprice, hedge, automate, or shift production
    SMEs Labor scarcity can support sales and local pricing Weak bargaining power, incomplete cost pass-through, higher wages and loan rates Compress margins, borrow, defer investment, or exit
    Banks Wider lending and securities margins Credit losses, deposit competition, JGB valuation effects Reprice loans, shorten duration, tighten selection
    State Higher nominal tax revenue Subsidy demands, tax relief, debt service Transfer private pressure into public borrowing

    The table describes tendencies, not fixed identities. A large retailer can be more exposed to yen depreciation than a small exporter. A highly liquid SME may be less rate-sensitive than a leveraged listed company. A pensioner with substantial deposits may gain interest income while another pensioner living primarily on the basic pension loses purchasing power. Firm size is a useful proxy for access to finance and bargaining power, but it is not a substitute for balance-sheet analysis.

    The mechanism nevertheless has a clear direction. When an external cost enters through the exchange rate or commodity prices, it must be absorbed by some combination of four margins. In stylized form:

    \[ \text{Imported cost shock} = \text{consumer price increase} + \text{corporate margin loss} + \text{wage restraint} + \text{fiscal compensation} \]

    The shares change over time. Firms may initially absorb a shock, later pass it to consumers, and then seek relief or tax support when demand weakens. Workers may secure a nominal wage increase only after the earlier price rise has reduced real income. Government subsidies may suppress measured CPI temporarily while preserving the underlying import cost in the public budget. The adjustment is not avoided; it is reassigned and delayed.

    The July 2026 BOJ outlook described this sequence in unusually direct terms. Higher crude-oil prices were expected to reduce corporate profits and household real income through a deterioration in the terms of trade. Government relief and wage increases would support activity, but private consumption was expected to remain roughly flat because of higher prices, especially energy. At the same time, the Bank expected recent yen depreciation and higher input costs to lift inflation again in the second half of FY2026.2

    That is not a conventional wage-price spiral in which demand expands without constraint. It is a contest over who can pass a cost onward.

    6.2 Headline inflation conceals the distributional burden¶

    Japan’s national consumer price index rose 1.7 percent from a year earlier in June 2026. The index excluding fresh food rose 1.6 percent, while the index excluding fresh food and energy rose 1.7 percent. Those readings were below the inflation rates that had dominated the previous several years and, viewed alone, suggested that the immediate cost-of-living shock had eased.3

    The composition was less benign. Food excluding fresh food rose 3.1 percent and contributed 0.78 percentage point to the annual headline increase. Kerosene rose 16.5 percent and propane 3.9 percent. Energy as a category was almost flat partly because electricity, city-gas, and gasoline prices were affected by government support and tax measures. The Statistics Bureau estimated that gasoline and fuel policies reduced the energy contribution to the index by 0.74 percentage point.4

    This creates a measurement problem with political consequences. The CPI correctly measures the price of a representative basket; it does not claim that every household purchases that basket. A household’s effective inflation rate is closer to:

    \[ \pi_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\pi_j \]

    where \(w_{ij}\) is household \(i\)’s expenditure share on category \(j\), and \(\pi_j\) is that category’s price change. A lower-income household devoting a larger share to food and utilities can experience an effective inflation rate above the national headline. A household owning equities, holding substantial foreign assets, or receiving a strong wage settlement can offset that burden. A pensioner renting in a low-cost region but heating with kerosene faces a different basket from a salaried household in Tokyo.

    The difference between measured and experienced inflation does not mean the CPI is false. It means distribution matters. Food accounted for 28.3 percent of consumption expenditure among two-or-more-person households in 2024, up from 26.6 percent in 2022. Real household consumption fell in both 2023 and 2024 even as nominal expenditure rose.5 By mid-2026, slower headline inflation had not reversed the accumulated increase in the price level. A lower inflation rate means prices are rising more slowly; it does not restore the purchasing power lost during the earlier increase.

    Subsidies further complicate the signal. When the state reduces electricity, gas, gasoline, or kerosene prices, measured CPI falls and real household income improves relative to the no-policy counterfactual. That relief is economically real. The cost has not disappeared; it has moved from the utility bill or fuel pump into government expenditure or forgone tax revenue. Chapter 8 examines the political structure of that transfer. For domestic transmission, the key point is that household inflation and fiscal pressure are partially interchangeable.

    The weak yen amplifies the problem even when international commodity prices stabilize. Importers purchase energy, food inputs, fertilizer, feed, machinery, and intermediate goods in foreign currency. A decline in the dollar price of a commodity can be offset by a weaker exchange rate or by the expense of securing alternative transport routes. The BOJ’s July outlook explicitly warned that Japanese procurement costs might not fall as quickly as international oil prices because of transportation and supply-chain costs.6

    The household burden is therefore not captured by asking whether national CPI is above or below 2 percent in one month. The relevant questions are whether essential categories continue to outpace income, whether policy support is suppressing the observed rate, and whether the cumulative price level has permanently altered consumption behavior.

    6.3 Real wages improved, but the breadth of the improvement remains the test¶

    The strongest evidence against a one-sided household-crisis narrative is the wage data. In May 2026, total cash earnings at establishments with at least five employees rose 3.2 percent from a year earlier to 311,165 yen. Scheduled cash earnings rose 3.0 percent, and contractual base pay rose by the same rate. After adjustment using the CPI excluding imputed rent, real cash earnings rose 1.4 percent. This was the fifth consecutive month of positive real-wage growth.7

    The improvement matters. Sustained positive real wages would weaken a central part of the slow-compression thesis by allowing household demand to recover without additional fiscal compensation. It would also give the BOJ greater room to normalize rates because tighter policy would be transmitted into an economy with rising real income rather than continuing purchasing-power losses.

    The data also reveal why one positive aggregate cannot settle the question. Full-time employees’ total earnings rose 3.5 percent in May, while part-time employees’ earnings rose only 1.5 percent. Hourly scheduled pay for part-time workers rose 4.9 percent, but monthly earnings were constrained by hours. The common-establishment series, which reduces distortions from changes in the survey sample, showed total earnings up 2.9 percent and scheduled earnings up 2.5 percent. The official release also cautioned that preliminary results can be revised and that a partial rotation of sampled establishments in January 2026 created a discontinuity.8

    The annual spring wage negotiations show a similar divide. Rengo’s final 2026 tally covered 5,368 union settlements and produced a weighted average increase of 5.01 percent, including regular seniority-related increments. Unions at firms with fewer than 300 workers secured 4.69 percent. The explicitly identified base-pay component was 3.50 percent overall and 3.51 percent among the smaller-union group.9

    Those results are materially stronger than the wage settlements of the low-inflation era. They also cover fewer than three million union members in a labor force of roughly 70 million. Rengo’s small-union results are not a census of all SME workers. They are evidence that wage pressure has spread beyond the largest corporations, not proof that every employer can finance the same increase.

    Three distinctions should therefore govern the wage analysis:

    1. Negotiated wage increases are not identical to economy-wide realized earnings. Annual increments, base pay, bonuses, hours, and workforce composition produce different measures.
    2. Nominal wage growth is not the same as purchasing-power growth. Real income depends on the household’s price basket, taxes, social-insurance contributions, and employment stability.
    3. A wage increase is simultaneously household income and a firm cost. Whether it strengthens the economy depends on productivity and price pass-through.

    The third distinction is the most important. A firm that raises pay by 5 percent while increasing value added per worker can support both wages and profits. A firm that raises pay because it cannot recruit workers, while input costs and borrowing rates also rise, may finance the increase from margins or debt. The same wage statistic can therefore represent a virtuous cycle in one company and a solvency squeeze in another.

    The correct test is not whether the 2026 shuntō was strong. It was. The test is whether realized base-pay gains remain above household-specific inflation for several years and spread across nonunion workers, part-time workers, service-sector employees, and firms without strong pricing power. Until that breadth is established, the wage recovery is a stabilizer with incomplete coverage.

    6.4 Household consumption is the first shock absorber¶

    Households can respond to a loss of purchasing power by reducing savings, borrowing, working more, substituting cheaper products, or cutting consumption. Japan’s recent pattern shows substantial use of the last two margins.

    In May 2026, average consumption expenditure among two-or-more-person households was 320,345 yen, 1.3 percent higher in nominal terms but 0.4 percent lower in real terms than a year earlier. Workers’ households recorded monthly income of 534,893 yen, up 2.4 percent nominally and 0.7 percent in real terms.10 The combination is consistent with improving income and restrained consumption rather than a household sector already entering an expansionary wage-demand cycle.

    One month cannot establish a structural trend. The Family Income and Expenditure Survey is volatile, and household composition changes over time. The result nevertheless fits the longer pattern of elevated food shares, weak sentiment, and recurring government support. On July 31, the BOJ characterized private consumption as resilient but acknowledged weakness in household sentiment. Its forward baseline was more revealing: consumption would be roughly flat in FY2026 because higher energy prices offset wage gains and government transfers.11

    The Cabinet Office had reached a similar conclusion one day earlier. It reduced its FY2026 real-growth estimate from 1.3 to 0.9 percent and its private-consumption forecast from 1.3 to 0.9 percent while increasing its CPI assumption to 2.2 percent. The revision attributed part of the deterioration to higher energy costs.12

    Consumption restraint is a powerful stabilizer in the short run. It reduces imports, prevents household leverage from accelerating, and allows firms to clear cost shocks without an immediate wage-price explosion. It is also a drag on domestic demand. When households protect their balance sheets by cutting discretionary purchases, domestically oriented businesses lose revenue, which weakens their ability to raise wages and invest.

    This is why the phrase “households have large savings” is insufficient. Aggregate household financial assets reached 2,386 trillion yen at the end of March 2026, including 1,126 trillion yen in currency and deposits.13 That stock gives the household sector enormous resilience. It does not reveal which households own the assets, which are willing to spend them, or whether older savers will dissave in response to inflation. The existence of wealth is not the same as a high propensity to consume.

    Equity gains create a similar asymmetry. Higher share prices improve wealth for asset owners and support consumption at the margin. Households without meaningful equity exposure receive little direct benefit but still pay higher prices. The BOJ’s projection that wealth effects would support FY2026 consumption is plausible at the aggregate level; its distribution is necessarily unequal.

    The domestic regime can therefore remain stable while consumption underperforms income. Households absorb uncertainty by saving the wage gain, substituting within the basket, or accepting lower real discretionary consumption. That response limits immediate instability and weakens the demand needed to complete the government’s intended wage-price-growth cycle.

    6.5 Corporate Japan is not one balance sheet¶

    Corporate aggregates have been one of the strongest buffers in the Japanese system. At the end of March 2026, private nonfinancial corporations held 1,829 trillion yen in financial assets, including 367 trillion yen in currency and deposits and 285 trillion yen in outward direct investment. The sector had accumulated substantial liquidity, foreign assets, and equity stakes over the low-rate period.14

    That aggregate supports several valid counterarguments. Many firms can absorb higher interest costs. Large manufacturers can hedge currency exposure, issue bonds, shift production, and finance automation. All else equal, net foreign-currency earnings rise in yen terms when the currency weakens. Corporate Japan is not approaching a generalized balance-sheet crisis.

    The same aggregate obscures three forms of segmentation.

    First, foreign-currency exposure is not uniform. A company earning dollars abroad and bearing much of its cost base in yen may receive a translation and margin benefit from depreciation. An importer selling into a price-sensitive domestic market receives the opposite shock. The June 2026 Tankan showed that firms assumed an average FY2026 exchange rate of 152.57 yen per dollar. By late July, the market had traded near 164 before intervention. The gap can improve reported yen earnings for net exporters while raising unhedged procurement costs for importers.15

    Second, pricing power differs by position in the supply chain. A branded manufacturer or dominant retailer can negotiate procurement terms and change selling prices. A subcontractor competing for a small number of buyers may be unable to pass through labor, materials, and energy costs without risking the relationship. The cost is then retained inside the subcontractor’s margin.

    Third, investment capacity differs sharply by size. The June Tankan projected FY2026 fixed investment including land purchases to rise 11.5 percent at large enterprises and 6.0 percent at medium-sized enterprises. Small enterprises projected an 8.3 percent decline, driven by a 13.0 percent decline among small nonmanufacturing firms. When software, research and development, and other fixed investment were included while land was excluded, small enterprises still projected a 1.8 percent decline, against a 10.7 percent increase at large firms.16

    This divergence matters more than the national capital-expenditure total. Large-company spending on AI, semiconductors, data centers, automation, and overseas capacity can raise aggregate investment while the domestic supplier base defers replacement equipment and productivity improvements. The result may increase the productivity gap that initially caused the wage and margin divide.

    The Tankan also demonstrated that favorable sentiment and weaker profits can coexist. In June, the business-conditions index stood at +22 for large manufacturers and +37 for large nonmanufacturers, compared with +9 and +15 for small firms. Yet enterprises expected current profits to fall 6.5 percent in FY2026. Small manufacturers forecast an 11.1 percent decline and small nonmanufacturers an 8.8 percent decline. Large manufacturers and nonmanufacturers forecast declines of 6.7 and 6.3 percent respectively.17

    The forecast was not evidence of collapse. Profit levels remained high, and firms often submit conservative early-year plans. It was evidence that revenue growth, favorable sentiment, and profit compression can occur simultaneously when input and wage costs rise. Corporate health must therefore be evaluated by distribution and margin, not only by the aggregate profit stock.

    6.6 SMEs sit at the intersection of every domestic pressure¶

    Small and medium-sized enterprises account for 99.7 percent of Japanese firms and roughly 70 percent of employment.18 They are not a peripheral sector that can be allowed to adjust without macroeconomic consequence. At the same time, “SME” describes millions of firms ranging from productive exporters and specialist manufacturers to marginal local operators. Any claim that the entire sector is fragile is analytically empty.

    The more defensible claim is that SMEs are disproportionately represented at the point where four pressures meet:

    • rising imported materials and energy costs;
    • pressure to raise wages in a labor shortage;
    • incomplete ability to raise selling prices;
    • increasing bank-loan rates.

    The March 2026 price-negotiation survey conducted by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry found that SMEs had passed through 54.2 percent of their total cost increase, up by roughly one percentage point from the previous survey. Pass-through was 55.7 percent for raw materials and 50.0 percent for labor costs.19

    An average pass-through rate above 50 percent is evidence of improvement. It also means that nearly half of the measured cost increase remained somewhere other than the selling price. Some was offset through productivity, substitution, or lower procurement expense. The residual was absorbed through profit margins, lower owner income, wage restraint, reduced investment, or additional borrowing.

    The supply-chain position matters. METI found that first-tier contractors achieved pass-through above 50 percent, while rates declined further down the subcontracting chain. This is the mechanism behind the claim that large firms can push pressure downward without explicitly ordering suppliers to accept a loss. A buyer can demand quality, delivery, and wage compliance while resisting a price increase. Each demand is rational in isolation; together they force the supplier to finance the gap.

    The June Tankan captures the same asymmetry through input and output prices. For large manufacturers, the diffusion index for rising input prices was +62 and the index for rising output prices +40, a 22-point gap. For small manufacturers, the input index was +76 and the output index +40, a 36-point gap. Small manufacturers expected the input index to reach +77 and output prices +52 in September.20

    These diffusion indices do not measure the percentage size of price changes and cannot be converted directly into margins. They show the breadth of firms reporting increases. The much larger input-output gap among small manufacturers is consistent with weaker cost recovery.

    Labor scarcity intensifies the collision. The Tankan employment-conditions index was −39 for small enterprises in June and was expected to fall to −43 in September, indicating a large excess of firms reporting insufficient labor over those reporting excess employment. In such a market, a small employer must raise wages to retain staff even if sales and productivity do not support the increase.

    This creates the SME wage trap:

    Do not raise wages
    → lose workers and productive capacity.
    Raise wages without productivity or pass-through
    → lose margin and weaken debt-service capacity.
    Raise prices fully
    → risk losing customers or contracts.
    Borrow to bridge the gap
    → become more exposed to BOJ normalization.

    The trap does not imply that every firm should survive. Business exit reallocates workers and capital, and Japan has long retained firms whose low productivity was sustained by cheap credit. The relevant issue is transition capacity. If stronger firms can acquire assets, workers can move, and local services remain available, exit may raise productivity. If closures are concentrated in rural construction, transport, healthcare, food service, and retail where replacement capacity is scarce, “creative destruction” can become service withdrawal rather than renewal.

    6.7 Credit stress appears first in the vulnerable tail¶

    The BOJ’s April 2026 Financial System Report offers an important restraint on the argument. It found that the corporate sector’s resilience to higher rates had improved: the share of effectively debt-free firms was rising, the share of firms whose borrowing exceeded cash and deposits was falling, interest-coverage ratios had improved, and bankruptcies directly attributed to rate increases remained minimal.21

    That evidence rejects the claim that a 1 percent policy rate had already produced generalized corporate credit distress. It also identifies where the next stage would appear. Defaults remained concentrated among firms that had entered the pandemic with operating losses or negative net worth, with an additional elevated group among firms that became impaired during the pandemic. Higher labor and raw-material costs added pressure to these already weak balance sheets.

    The lending-rate transmission was visible. Among SMEs borrowing mainly from regional banks, floating-rate loans repriced more rapidly than fixed-rate loans between March 2024 and September 2025. The BOJ found that a rate increase of at least 15 basis points was associated with only a limited overall change in defaults; the statistically significant increase appeared among firms combining negative net worth with operating losses.22

    This is the expected pattern in a gradual normalization. The first rate increases do not cause a broad failure wave. They remove margin from borrowers already dependent on rollover, weak profits, or principal deferral. Credit risk emerges as a tail event before it becomes an aggregate event.

    Private bankruptcy data show that the tail was expanding by mid-2026. Tokyo Shoko Research recorded 5,346 bankruptcies involving liabilities of at least 10 million yen in the first half of the year, 7.1 percent more than a year earlier and the first first-half total above 5,000 in twelve years. Firms with liabilities below 100 million yen accounted for 77.0 percent of cases; 90.6 percent employed fewer than ten workers. TSR classified all of the failures as SMEs under the statutory definition.23

    The composition matters more than the total. Labor-shortage-related bankruptcies reached 237, including 120 attributed to rising labor costs. Price-related bankruptcies reached 439. Those categories are based on case classifications and should not be added as mutually exclusive causal estimates. A failed firm can face several pressures simultaneously. They nevertheless show that the labor and inflation channels identified in the Tankan were appearing in actual exits.

    The aggregate credit signal remained stable. The vulnerable tail did not. That combination is characteristic of slow compression: enough firms and banks remain healthy to prevent a system-wide event, while the cost of adjustment is concentrated in smaller entities with limited replacement capacity.

    6.8 Banks gain from normalization before they absorb its credit losses¶

    Higher interest rates are not unambiguously harmful to Japanese banks. After decades of compressed lending margins, normalization can improve income on loans and newly purchased securities. The April 2026 Financial System Report found that the rise in asset yields was improving bank profitability as loans and securities repriced. Deposit rates—particularly at regional and shinkin banks—had risen more slowly than lending rates.24

    That sequence produces an initial earnings benefit:

    \[ \Delta \text{net interest income} \approx \Delta \text{asset yield} - \Delta \text{funding cost} - \Delta \text{credit loss} \]

    The first two terms were favorable through early 2026. The third operates with a lag. A borrower’s loan can reprice immediately or at the next reset, while default occurs only after cash reserves, owner support, refinancing, and payment deferrals are exhausted.

    Banks also differ. Major institutions possess diversified fee income, global operations, market hedges, and larger capital buffers. Regional banks depend more heavily on local lending and securities income. Shinkin banks operate in even narrower markets. Higher rates can improve all three groups’ margins while affecting their securities portfolios, deposit competition, and borrower quality differently.

    The BOJ judged the system stable as a whole. Its stress tests found that banks retained sufficient capital to withstand a global-financial-crisis-scale scenario and a compound scenario involving geopolitical stress, higher oil prices, falling asset values, and substantially higher interest rates.25 This finding should anchor the baseline. The monograph does not forecast an imminent Japanese banking crisis.

    Aggregate capital resilience does not eliminate concentration risk. The July 2026 failure of payment processor Zentoshin illustrates the distinction. Teikoku Databank reported liabilities of approximately 125.9 billion yen, the largest Japanese corporate failure of the year at that point. Towa Bank disclosed 8.0 billion yen of loans to the company, equal to 8.83 percent of its consolidated net assets, with 5.886 billion yen not covered by collateral or prior provisions.26

    Zentoshin did not fail because the BOJ raised rates to 1 percent. Its collapse followed operating losses, misconduct, and a loss of creditor confidence. It should not be presented as proof of the monetary thesis. It demonstrates a different point: a single idiosyncratic borrower can create a material charge for a regional institution, and the same failure can interrupt cash flow for small merchants using the borrower’s payment services.

    That network structure is what makes regional credit transmission important. A bank can be adequately capitalized and still respond to a material loss by becoming more selective, shortening duration, reducing exposure to weaker borrowers, or protecting margins. Each response is prudent for the institution. Collectively, those responses reduce the amount of cheap, patient credit available to the firms least able to access capital markets.

    The banking risk is therefore not simply insolvency. It is a change in intermediation behavior. A system can remain well capitalized while becoming less willing to refinance the marginal borrower.

    6.9 Housing converts rate normalization into a delayed household charge¶

    Housing is the clearest household example of gradual repricing. Floating-rate mortgages account for the majority of Japanese housing loans. Following the July 2024 policy increase, many applied rates rose by 0.15 percentage point; after the January 2025 increase, they rose by another 0.25 point. The BOJ expected further increases after the December 2025 hike.27

    Payment rules delay the household effect. Many mortgages apply a five-year rule that holds the monthly payment constant between reset dates even when the applied interest rate changes. The unpaid adjustment changes the allocation between principal and interest and appears in the monthly payment only later. The BOJ estimated that only around 20 percent of relevant loans experience a payment revision in a given year.

    Where payment changes occurred, the increase was generally 1,000–3,000 yen per month in FY2024. By FY2025, more loans were experiencing increases around 4,000–6,000 yen, and applied rates on many floating loans exceeded 1 percent. Delinquency remained low and stable.28

    The more structural signal was maturity extension. By September 2025, mortgages with original maturities above 35 years accounted for more than half of newly originated floating-rate loan value at regional banks; maturities above 40 years accounted for more than 10 percent. Banks increasingly offered forty- and fifty-year loans. The BOJ concluded that super-long terms were being used to keep monthly payments within a manageable range as property prices and borrowing amounts rose.29

    Long maturities are not inherently unsound. They can match payments to lifetime income and improve housing access. They also make the household more sensitive to future rates by keeping principal outstanding for longer. The borrower protects current cash flow by exchanging it for duration.

    This mirrors the sovereign mechanism in Chapter 4. The government delays higher interest expense through long debt maturities; the household delays higher mortgage payments through contractual reset rules and longer amortization. In both cases, repricing is gradual enough to prevent an immediate break and persistent enough to narrow future flexibility.

    Housing also reveals a generational asymmetry. Existing owners with fixed low-rate loans or no mortgage receive a different outcome from younger buyers entering at higher property prices, larger principals, and longer maturities. Aggregate household wealth can rise because housing and equities appreciate while market entry becomes more expensive. The same asset price that strengthens one balance sheet raises the required leverage of another.

    6.10 Pensioners and savers receive only partial protection¶

    Rate normalization creates a legitimate benefit for savers. Japanese households held 1,126 trillion yen in currency and deposits at the end of March 2026, 47.2 percent of household financial assets. As deposit and bond rates rise, some of the income previously transferred from savers to borrowers under near-zero rates returns to households.30

    The benefit is gradual and uneven. Deposit rates at regional and shinkin banks had risen more slowly than loan rates through early 2026. Cash holders receive little benefit until banks reprice deposits or funds move into higher-yielding products. Households with equities and foreign assets gain differently from those holding ordinary deposits. A household with a mortgage can pay a higher lending rate before receiving a meaningful increase on its transaction balance.

    Pension indexation provides a second form of partial protection. For FY2026, the government used a 2.1 percent nominal take-home-wage reference rather than the 3.2 percent price increase because the statutory formula applies the lower measure when prices exceed the wage benchmark. It then applied the macroeconomic slide. The basic-pension adjustment was 1.9 percent and the earnings-related pension adjustment 2.0 percent.31

    The mechanism is deliberate. It protects pension-system sustainability by preventing benefits from automatically rising as fast as prices when the supporting workforce and wage base do not. It also means that inflation can reduce pensioners’ real purchasing power even while nominal benefits increase.

    This is not an accidental policy failure. It is the distributional form of demographic adjustment. Current pensioners absorb part of the system’s financing pressure through indexation below the relevant price increase; current workers absorb it through contributions and taxes; future retirees absorb it through lower replacement rates if the macroeconomic slide continues.

    The effect differs by household. A pensioner with deposits, equities, and an owner-occupied home may offset benefit compression through asset income. A pensioner relying primarily on the basic pension has far less room. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare estimated typical FY2026 monthly benefits of approximately 63,513 yen for a man whose career was centered on National Pension category-one coverage and 61,771 yen for a similarly situated woman, compared with 176,793 yen and 134,640 yen for men and women with long Employees’ Pension coverage.32

    These are stylized career categories, not the full distribution. They show why the same CPI and pension adjustment produce different levels of hardship. A policy regime that relies on gradual real benefit restraint can remain fiscally manageable while increasing demand for targeted cash support, healthcare relief, and food assistance. That demand then returns to the fiscal channel.

    6.11 Compensation closes the domestic-fiscal loop¶

    The transmission mechanism eventually returns to the state. When households reduce consumption, SMEs lose margin, energy-intensive firms face cash-flow pressure, or pensioners fall behind essential prices, the political response is rarely to allow the entire adjustment to occur immediately. Governments subsidize electricity and gas, reduce fuel taxes, transfer cash, guarantee loans, support wage increases, and create refinancing facilities.

    Those interventions can be economically justified. They prevent temporary commodity shocks from causing permanent firm closures and protect households that cannot smooth consumption through savings. They can also stabilize inflation expectations by interrupting the visible rise in administered prices.

    The fiscal consequence depends on design. Temporary, targeted, funded relief can bridge a genuine shock. Repeated, broad, debt-financed relief converts the private cost into a public obligation. The household receives a lower utility bill; the state receives a higher financing requirement. The firm preserves employment; the budget absorbs the credit or subsidy risk.

    This creates the domestic-fiscal loop:

    Weak yen and imported inflation
    → lower household real income and SME margins
    → weaker consumption and greater bankruptcy risk
    → subsidies, tax relief, transfers, and credit support
    → larger fiscal commitment
    → higher issuance or reduced fiscal room
    → greater constraint on BOJ normalization
    → persistent yen pressure.

    The loop does not mean relief is always counterproductive. It means the relief must be included in the cost of preserving the regime. Excluding it produces a misleading comparison in which households appear resilient and the fiscal position appears unchanged.

    The July 2026 data make that interaction visible. Fuel and utility measures lowered national CPI relative to the no-policy counterfactual. Household consumption was roughly flat or declining in real terms. The BOJ expected government transfers to help maintain consumption. The Cabinet Office nevertheless reduced its growth forecast because higher energy costs were weakening household and corporate income. The stabilizer and the cost operated at the same time.

    6.12 Counterarguments and limits¶

    Six counterarguments qualify this chapter’s findings.

    First, real wages have already turned positive. Correct. Five consecutive months of positive real earnings through May 2026 were a material improvement. If broad real wage growth persists, private consumption strengthens, and the gains spread beyond unionized and full-time workers, household compression will ease. The chapter treats this as a live falsification condition, not a statistical inconvenience.

    Second, corporate Japan holds extraordinary cash and foreign assets. Correct. Aggregate corporate liquidity is a central reason a gradual rate increase need not cause widespread defaults. The argument concerns distribution: the firms with the largest buffers and strongest foreign earnings are not necessarily those employing workers in import-intensive local services or operating at the bottom of supply chains.

    Third, higher rates restore income to savers and improve bank profitability. Correct. Normalization reverses part of the long suppression of deposit and bond income. The net domestic effect depends on the speed at which deposit rates rise relative to lending rates, who owns the assets, and whether later credit losses offset the initial improvement in margins.

    Fourth, rising bankruptcies may represent healthy restructuring. Partly correct. Exit is necessary in an economy with labor scarcity and low productivity. Bankruptcy counts alone cannot distinguish productive reallocation from the loss of essential local capacity. The thesis is weakened if workers and assets move rapidly into more productive firms without reducing regional service availability or requiring repeated fiscal support.

    Fifth, price pass-through is improving. Correct. The 54.2 percent SME pass-through rate in March 2026 was an improvement, and the strong output-price intentions in the Tankan show firms adapting to inflation. Successful pass-through can restore margins. It also transfers the cost to consumers, which means it resolves the firm problem only if wages and productivity rise sufficiently to preserve demand.

    Sixth, the banking system is resilient. Correct. BOJ stress tests and current default data do not support an imminent systemic banking crisis. The relevant risk is narrower: individual exposures, gradual securities repricing, deposit competition, and more selective credit can tighten financing conditions without threatening the system as a whole.

    There are also measurement limits. Monthly wage and consumption surveys are volatile and subject to revision. Rengo data cover union settlements rather than the entire labor market. Bankruptcy classifications are produced by private credit-research firms and identify proximate causes, not econometric causality. Tankan diffusion indices measure the balance of responses, not the size of price or labor changes. Aggregate household and corporate financial assets reveal sectoral capacity but not ownership distribution.

    These limitations argue against deterministic claims. They do not erase the common direction across independent data: essential prices rose faster than headline CPI, wage gains varied by employment type and coverage, small firms faced a larger input-output price gap, loan rates were rising, mortgage maturities were lengthening, and failures were concentrated among very small enterprises.

    6.13 Finding: aggregate resilience is financed by unequal absorption¶

    Japan’s domestic economy retained substantial resilience at the July 2026 cutoff. Real wages had improved, household financial assets were large, corporate liquidity remained strong, lending continued, and banks held sufficient aggregate capital. A research monograph that ignores those facts would confuse pressure with collapse.

    The same evidence shows how the regime remains stable.

    Households absorb import inflation through lower discretionary consumption.
    Large firms use foreign earnings, pricing power, hedges, and investment capacity.
    SMEs absorb unrecovered materials, labor, and interest costs through margins and borrowing.
    Banks initially gain from asset repricing, then selectively absorb credit and securities losses.
    Mortgage borrowers extend duration and receive payment increases with a lag.
    Pensioners receive nominal protection below the full price benchmark.
    The state compensates the most visible losses and moves part of the burden into future budgets.

    This allocation prevents one pressure from becoming an immediate national rupture. It also obstructs the intended transition from inflation to wages to consumption to investment. A household that saves its wage increase, an SME that finances higher pay from margin, and a bank that raises lending rates faster than deposit rates can all appear stable individually. Collectively, they produce weak domestic demand and repeated calls for fiscal support.

    The domestic core of slow compression is therefore not universal impoverishment or corporate failure. It is the widening separation between the sectors that can reprice and the sectors that must absorb. As long as large balance sheets, public transfers, and gradual contract repricing remain available, the system can continue. Its economic cost appears as lower consumption, deferred investment, longer debt duration, business exit, and a larger fiscal role in maintaining private stability.

    Chapter 7 examines the force that makes this allocation progressively harder to sustain. Demographic contraction reduces the number of workers over whom wage, tax, pension, and credit adjustments can be distributed. It turns today’s labor shortage into tomorrow’s tax-base problem, narrows the pool of local customers and borrowers, and weakens the domestic savings structure that supported both banks and the JGB market. Domestic transmission determines who carries the current burden; demography determines how many balance sheets remain available to carry it.
