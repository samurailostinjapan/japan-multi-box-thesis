# Chapter 5 — The Bank of Japan, the JGB Market, and the Yen

The Bank of Japan can raise its policy rate. It can purchase government bonds when market functioning deteriorates. The Ministry of Finance can sell foreign-currency assets and buy yen. Japan therefore retains powerful monetary and financial instruments. The problem is not the disappearance of those instruments. It is the declining possibility that any one of them can be used without impairing the objective of another.

The monetary collision can be stated simply. If the Bank tightens policy to contain inflation and support confidence in the currency, it raises the marginal cost of public refinancing and private credit. If it delays tightening to protect borrowers, banks, and the fiscal position, the interest-rate differential remains wide, the yen bears more of the adjustment, and imported inflation intensifies. If the Ministry of Finance intervenes to interrupt depreciation without a corresponding change in the policy mix, it can force a sharp repositioning but cannot by itself alter the expected return on holding yen. If the Bank responds to rising JGB yields with larger purchases, it can stabilize the bond market while weakening the signal that monetary restraint will be sustained.

This is not a claim that Japan has lost monetary control. The BOJ still sets the overnight rate, and its balance sheet remains large enough to alter the liquidity and pricing of the JGB market. Nor does every rise in JGB yields represent fiscal distress. Higher yields are a normal part of leaving a zero-rate regime, and a more active market can improve price discovery. The narrower argument is that normalization has become internally asymmetric. Relatively small increases in the policy rate can transmit meaningfully into fiscal and domestic-credit conditions while remaining too small, relative to foreign rates and Japan’s risk premium, to generate durable yen appreciation.

The yen is the pressure valve through which that asymmetry becomes visible. It converts a debate about the neutral rate, bond purchases, and future refinancing into current import prices and household politics. The JGB curve records the expected cost of normalization. The currency records whether markets believe normalization is sufficient. By mid-2026, those markets were no longer delivering the same verdict.

## 5.1 Normalization has moved beyond symbolism

The BOJ ended its negative interest-rate policy and formal yield-curve-control framework in March 2024. It subsequently raised the overnight-rate target to approximately 0.25 percent in July 2024, 0.5 percent in January 2025, 0.75 percent in December 2025, and 1.0 percent in June 2026.[^1] The sequence was gradual, but by June 2026 it could no longer be dismissed as a merely symbolic departure from exceptional easing. The policy rate had reached its highest level in thirty-one years, the complementary deposit facility paid 1.0 percent, and the basic loan rate stood at 1.25 percent.[^2]

The importance of this transition lies less in the historical comparison than in its interaction with a balance sheet constructed during the zero-rate era. A one-percent policy rate is low in absolute terms and remained below most estimates of Japan’s nominal neutral rate. It is nevertheless materially different from a rate at or below zero when applied to floating-rate corporate loans, variable-rate mortgages, bank deposit pricing, money-market instruments, and the expected path of government-bond coupons.

Normalization also operates through expectations. The policy rate affects the front of the JGB curve directly, but the rest of the curve embeds the expected future path of short rates, inflation, duration risk, supply, and the compensation investors require for holding government debt. The BOJ can therefore increase the overnight rate by 25 basis points while the ten- or thirty-year yield moves by more, less, or in the opposite direction. The curve is not a mechanical projection of the next policy meeting.

The June decision illustrated the dual character of normalization. The BOJ raised the overnight target from 0.75 to 1.0 percent while acknowledging that higher crude-oil prices were depressing activity even as they increased price pressure. Tightening was intended to prevent a supply and exchange-rate shock from becoming embedded in underlying inflation. It could not produce more energy, improve Japan’s terms of trade, or reverse the fiscal support used to shelter households from that shock.

This distinction matters because the same headline inflation can call for conflicting policy responses. If inflation reflects stronger domestic demand and rising real incomes, higher rates restrain excess demand. If it reflects imported energy, a weak yen, and supply-chain costs, higher rates may support the currency and expectations but also weaken already constrained domestic demand. The BOJ was not choosing between inflation control and no inflation control. It was choosing which transmission channel would bear the adjustment.

## 5.2 The neutral rate is a range, not an escape route

The concept of a neutral interest rate is often treated as if it identifies a safe terminal point. It does not. A neutral rate is an unobservable estimate of the rate consistent with the economy operating neither above nor below potential after temporary shocks have passed. It changes with productivity, demography, saving, investment, fiscal policy, and inflation expectations. In Japan, decades of near-zero policy rates make the estimate unusually uncertain because the available data contain little recent evidence of how the economy behaves under sustained positive rates.

BOJ estimates published in 2026 placed the natural real rate between approximately minus 0.9 and plus 0.5 percent. Adding the Bank’s 2 percent inflation objective produced a nominal neutral-rate range of approximately 1.1–2.5 percent. Board member Kazuyuki Masu emphasized that the range was a reference, not a precise destination, and that the effects on prices, employment, and financial conditions had to be observed as the Bank moved toward it.[^3]

That uncertainty has two implications. First, a policy rate of 1 percent may still be accommodative in aggregate. The BOJ’s July outlook stated that real rates remained negative, especially in the short- to medium-term zone, and that financial conditions continued to support activity.[^4] Second, the upper end of a statistical neutral-rate range does not establish what the fiscal and political system can absorb. A rate can be neutral for aggregate demand while being highly restrictive for leveraged SMEs, recent mortgage borrowers, weak regional firms, or a government refinancing more than one hundred trillion yen of maturing debt in some years.

The monograph therefore distinguishes three concepts:

| Concept | Meaning | Status at the July 31, 2026 cutoff |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated nominal neutral range | Model-based rate consistent with economy-wide balance | Approximately 1.1–2.5% |
| Policy rate | BOJ’s operating target for the overnight call rate | 1.0% |
| Practical compression zone | Range in which side effects may become politically or financially binding before aggregate neutrality is reached | Hypothesis centered on roughly 1.25–1.5% |

The third row is not an official BOJ estimate. It is a mechanism-based inference to be tested against the transmission evidence in Chapter 6. It does not imply that a move above 1.5 percent is technically impossible. It means that beyond approximately 1.25–1.5 percent, each additional increase is more likely to activate fiscal, credit, banking, housing, and political resistance. The practical stopping point can therefore sit below the statistically estimated neutral rate.

This gap is central to fiscal dominance. The BOJ need not receive an explicit instruction to hold rates below its preferred level. Anticipation of the consequences can narrow the feasible path before formal independence is tested. The constraint appears in meeting-to-meeting caution, increased attention to financial conditions, and a tendency to move in small increments while waiting for evidence that the previous increase has been absorbed.

## 5.3 Ending yield-curve control did not end bond-market management

The termination of formal yield-curve control restored a larger role for market pricing, but it did not turn the BOJ into an ordinary minority participant. At the end of March 2026, the Bank still held more than 40 percent of government bonds and treasury bills on a broad market-value measure. Its stock of JGBs continued to shape issue scarcity, liquidity, dealer inventories, and the sensitivity of yields to changes in purchase operations.[^5]

The BOJ’s own research documents the tradeoff. Bond purchases can improve liquidity around the issues being bought, but a sufficiently high ownership share removes securities from private circulation, reduces transaction volume, widens bid-ask spreads nonlinearly, and distorts the yield curve. Fixed-rate purchase operations can restrain yields while weakening the informational content of those yields.[^6] The old regime gained stability partly by reducing the market’s ability to express a price. Normalization requires the market to recover that function without being allowed to become disorderly.

The June 2026 purchase plan embodies that compromise. The Bank scheduled monthly purchases to decline from approximately 2.7 trillion yen in April–June 2026 to 2.5 trillion in July–September, 2.3 trillion in October–December, and 2.1 trillion in January–March 2027. From April 2027, purchases were to remain near 2 trillion yen per month. On the plan’s mechanical assumptions, the BOJ’s JGB holdings would decline from roughly 480 trillion yen at the end of March 2027 to 350–370 trillion yen at the end of March 2030.[^7]

This is quantitative tightening in stock terms, but not withdrawal from the market. Two trillion yen of monthly purchases still represents approximately 24 trillion yen per year. More important, the plan explicitly preserves the Bank’s authority to increase purchases, conduct fixed-rate operations, or supply funds against pooled collateral if long-term rates rise rapidly. The BOJ is simultaneously telling investors that long rates should be market formed and that it retains an open-ended capacity to interrupt that formation.

That ambiguity is intentional. A rigid withdrawal schedule could turn a liquidity event into a self-reinforcing selloff. A fully discretionary schedule would make private investors price not only inflation and supply, but the probability that the Bank changes the rules. The announced taper is designed to restore price discovery while keeping a latent ceiling on disorder.

The result is not formal yield-curve control. It is managed price discovery. The distinction is real: there is no published ten-year target, yields can rise substantially, and private buyers must absorb a growing marginal share. Yet the BOJ remains the market’s potential residual buyer. Investors therefore price both the sovereign and the reaction function of the central bank that owns a large part of it.

## 5.4 The curve contains several different policy verdicts

It is analytically misleading to speak of “the JGB yield” as if the curve expressed one risk. Different maturities respond to different mixtures of policy and balance-sheet pressure.

- The two-year sector is most sensitive to the expected BOJ policy path.
- The five-year sector combines that path with the early years of fiscal refinancing.
- The ten-year sector incorporates expected short rates, inflation, issuance, and term premium.
- The twenty- to forty-year sectors are particularly exposed to duration supply, insurer and pension demand, inflation uncertainty, and confidence in long-run fiscal management.

On July 9, 2026, the two-year yield stood near 1.445 percent and the five-year near 1.990 percent. The ten-year reached 2.900 percent, while the twenty-year reached 3.890 percent, the thirty-year 4.030 percent, and the forty-year 4.055 percent. The gap between ten- and two-year yields had widened to approximately 143 basis points, its largest since 2004.[^8]

That curve did not say simply that investors expected aggressive BOJ tightening. If that were the dominant message, the front end would have risen proportionally with the long end. Instead, the July steepening combined limited expectations for near-term policy increases with higher compensation for inflation, duration, issuance, and fiscal uncertainty farther out. The government could still fund itself, and auctions still attracted buyers. The price of doing so was changing.

The distinction between auction success and fiscal comfort is essential. A five-year auction on July 9 produced a bid-to-cover ratio of 3.43, stronger than the previous sale. That was evidence of functioning demand, not evidence that yields were harmless. Investors can buy enthusiastically after the market has repriced to a level they consider adequate. An auction that clears at a higher yield simultaneously confirms access to funding and establishes a higher marginal cost.

The curve can also move for reasons unrelated to Japanese fiscal credibility. Global bond yields, oil prices, hedging flows, dealer positioning, and expectations for the Federal Reserve affect JGBs. The July rise occurred amid renewed Middle East tension and higher energy prices. It would be incorrect to attribute every basis point to the Takaichi administration or to fiscal dominance.

The relevant evidence lies in the configuration rather than a single level: a high and steepening long end, a still-gradual policy path at the front, declining BOJ purchases, and a yen that remains weak despite higher domestic yields. Together, those conditions show that normalization is raising Japan’s financing cost faster than it is restoring monetary credibility.

## 5.5 The yen is the system’s pressure valve

The exchange rate absorbs the part of the monetary-fiscal imbalance that is not resolved through rates, taxes, or expenditure. When Japanese yields remain low relative to foreign yields, holding yen-funded positions remains comparatively attractive. When fiscal expansion raises doubts about the BOJ’s willingness to tighten, the expected future policy path can weaken the currency even before spending occurs. When imported energy prices rise, Japan’s terms of trade deteriorate and the private sector requires more foreign currency to pay for the same volume of imports.

The interest differential is therefore necessary to the explanation but not sufficient. At the July 31 cutoff, the BOJ’s policy rate was 1 percent while the Federal Reserve’s target range was 3.5–3.75 percent. A further 25-basis-point BOJ increase would narrow that overnight gap only modestly, particularly if the Federal Reserve also remained concerned about inflation.[^9] The exchange rate also reflected the expected duration of the gap, global risk appetite, commodity prices, Japanese capital outflows, hedging behavior, and a risk premium associated with the domestic policy mix.

The U.S. Treasury’s July 2026 foreign-exchange report offered unusually direct evidence. It described the yen as substantially undervalued after a 51 percent bilateral and real-effective depreciation from the end of 2011 to the end of April 2026. More important, it observed that yen weakness had persisted despite a narrowing U.S.–Japan interest-rate differential and argued that continued BOJ normalization would help anchor inflation expectations and reduce excessive exchange-rate volatility.[^10]

The persistence of depreciation as the gap narrowed does not prove that interest differentials ceased to matter. It shows that the market was pricing more than the current spread. A currency can weaken while its central bank tightens if the tightening path is already expected, if foreign policy tightens as well, or if investors conclude that the domestic central bank will stop before reaching a sufficiently restrictive rate.

This produces an asymmetric exchange-rate problem:

> A 25-basis-point hike can raise Japanese borrowing costs immediately.  
> The same hike may deliver little durable yen support if foreign rates remain high and the market expects the BOJ’s cycle to end early.

The weak yen then feeds back into the policy decision. It raises the yen price of energy, food, raw materials, semiconductors, and imported consumer goods. Firms pass some of those costs through. Households experience the result as a decline in purchasing power. Government responds with energy subsidies, tax relief, or transfers. Those measures weaken the fiscal signal and can increase doubts about how far the BOJ will be permitted to tighten. The pressure valve becomes a feedback loop.

The July BOJ outlook made this mechanism explicit. It expected headline inflation to accelerate clearly above 2 percent from the second half of FY2026, citing earlier crude-oil increases, wage-price pass-through, semiconductor prices associated with global AI demand, and recent yen depreciation. At the same time, government electricity, gas, and fuel measures lowered the measured FY2026 inflation path.[^11] Monetary and fiscal policy were acting on different layers of the same price shock: the BOJ sought to prevent underlying persistence while the government suppressed the household-facing price signal.

## 5.6 A rate hike can be both necessary and insufficient

The standard description of the BOJ’s choice—hike to strengthen the yen or hold to support growth—misses the possibility that a hike produces the costs of tightening without delivering the expected currency benefit. That possibility became visible after the June 2026 increase.

The BOJ raised the policy rate to 1 percent on June 16. By June 19, USD/JPY had reached 161.81, erasing the currency gains created by the record intervention campaign of late April and early May. Market participants still expected another BOJ increase by year-end, but the anticipated future hike did not reverse bearish yen positioning. Japanese rates remained far below U.S. rates, and the energy shock reinforced the import and inflation channel.[^12]

This is not evidence that the June hike weakened the yen. Counterfactual reasoning matters: without the increase, depreciation might have been faster. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion. A move from 0.75 to 1 percent was insufficient to change the direction of the currency under the prevailing global and fiscal conditions.

The same distinction applies to a move from 1 to 1.25 percent. Such an increase can improve the BOJ’s inflation credibility, reduce the expected depth of negative real rates, and disrupt some carry positions. It does not automatically close a policy-rate gap of more than two percentage points, change Japan’s energy-import requirement, or finance the government’s spending commitments. If the increase is fully anticipated, much of its direct currency effect can occur before the meeting.

The BOJ therefore faces a sequencing problem. Moving slowly allows each increase to be absorbed but gives the exchange rate time to test the next limit. Moving faster may surprise currency markets but increases the probability that credit and bond-market effects become politically binding. Waiting for broad evidence of demand-driven inflation can be prudent under uncertainty; waiting while the yen imports further inflation can also make the eventual adjustment larger.

The July 31 decision exposed this tension. The Policy Board voted eight to one to hold at 1 percent only six weeks after the June increase. Hajime Takata dissented in favor of 1.25 percent, arguing that overseas demand shocks and financial conditions had created a new phase of upside price risk. The accompanying outlook warned for the first time that underlying inflation could move above the 2 percent target, while Governor Kazuo Ueda stated that the Bank would debate future policy from the September meeting onward with greater attention to the risk of an overshoot.[^13]

The hold was not dovish in the ordinary sense. It preserved the current rate while deliberately increasing the probability assigned to a near-term hike. The front end of the JGB market responded accordingly. The currency’s response was less durable. That difference is the central event-level evidence of the chapter.

## 5.7 Intervention can change the path, not the regime

Foreign-exchange intervention is a Ministry of Finance decision executed by the BOJ as the minister’s agent. When supporting the yen, Japan sells foreign-currency assets held in the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account and buys yen. This is institutionally separate from the BOJ’s domestic monetary-policy decision, even though the central bank conducts the market operation.[^14]

Japan possessed approximately $1.287 trillion in official reserve assets at the end of June 2026. That stock gives the Ministry of Finance considerable intervention capacity.[^15] It does not make intervention costless or unlimited. The reserve portfolio serves external-liquidity and confidence functions, contains assets with different maturities and liquidity, and is linked to Foreign Exchange Fund financing bills. As Chapter 4 established, selling a foreign asset and retiring an associated liability can reduce gross balance-sheet size without creating net national wealth.

Intervention works through several channels:

1. **Market impact:** concentrated dollar selling can overwhelm liquidity and move the exchange rate sharply.
2. **Positioning:** leveraged yen shorts may be forced to close, extending the move.
3. **Signal:** the operation reveals the authorities’ tolerance for speed and disorder.
4. **Coordination:** foreign rate checks, aligned statements, or joint action can increase credibility.
5. **Time purchase:** authorities may bridge the period until monetary policy or external conditions turn more supportive.

The first two channels can be powerful even when fundamentals do not change. They are also prone to decay. Once leverage has been reduced and the official flow ends, investors reassess the same expected rates, fiscal policy, trade position, and risk premium that prevailed before the operation.

The institutional standard reinforces this limited objective. The September 2025 U.S.–Japan finance ministers’ statement affirmed that exchange rates should be market determined and that intervention, when considered, should be reserved for excessive volatility and disorderly movement rather than a competitive target level. It applied the standard symmetrically to excessive appreciation and depreciation.[^16] That language permits support for the yen while denying that the authorities are defending a permanent numerical floor.

In practice, markets infer reaction bands from behavior even when officials reject specific targets. Repeated action near a level can create a tactical ceiling for USD/JPY. That ceiling is not a new exchange-rate regime. It is a zone in which traders expect the state to increase the cost of testing the market.

The recent record demonstrates both the force and the limit of the instrument. Japan spent 11.7349 trillion yen on intervention between April 28 and May 27, 2026, the largest monthly amount on record.[^17] The operation drove USD/JPY from approximately 160.72 to 155.5 at its strongest point. By June 19 the pair had reached 161.81, wiping out the move. The intervention succeeded in creating a large temporary price adjustment and failed to reverse the underlying trend.

That is not the same as saying the money was wasted. A temporary interruption can reduce disorder, punish one-way leverage, slow pass-through, and create space for a policy meeting. Its effectiveness should be judged against those objectives. It becomes strategically inadequate when temporary containment is treated as a substitute for altering the policy mix that generates repeated pressure.

## 5.8 July 2026: a dual-market rejection

The events of July 30–31 provide the clearest compact example of the monetary-fiscal collision at the evidence cutoff.

During the week before the BOJ meeting, USD/JPY reached 163.99, near a forty-year extreme. On July 30, the dollar fell by as much as 3 percent to 158.34 in a short interval amid trading volumes far above normal. Reuters could not initially obtain official confirmation, but market participants identified the move as intervention; a subsequent report cited a market source saying Japan had bought yen in New York trading.[^18]

The first move was not sustained. By the Asian session on July 31, USD/JPY had returned to approximately 160.07. After the BOJ held the policy rate at 1 percent, it reached roughly 160.69. The market had absorbed a multi-yen official or authority-driven shock and then interpreted the unchanged rate as confirmation that the fundamental interest-rate gap remained.[^19]

The BOJ’s communication was nevertheless hawkish. It elevated the risk of underlying inflation overshooting 2 percent, cited the weak yen and AI-related price pressure, and opened the September meeting as a live point for renewed debate. Takata’s dissent supplied a concrete alternative of 1.25 percent. The policy message caused the two-year JGB yield to rise as traders increased the probability of further tightening.

The bond and currency markets were therefore not contradicting one another. They were pricing different questions:

| Market | Question being priced | July 31 message |
|---|---|---|
| Front-end JGBs | Will the BOJ raise rates again? | Increasingly plausible, potentially from September |
| Long-end JGBs | What compensation is required for inflation, duration, issuance, and fiscal risk? | Higher than under the old regime |
| Yen | Will the expected tightening path be sufficient relative to foreign rates and the policy mix? | Not yet |

The intervention story remained incomplete at the cutoff. BOJ money-market data implied a net funds outflow consistent with as much as $58.97 billion of dollar sales, but that figure was an estimate, not a published Ministry of Finance total. MOF’s July 31 monthly disclosure reported zero intervention from June 29 through July 29; by construction it did not cover July 30 or July 31. Later monthly and quarterly releases were required to establish the aggregate amount and operating dates.[^20]

The chapter therefore classifies the episode carefully:

> **Observed:** an abrupt multi-yen move on July 30, exceptional volume, rapid retracement, a BOJ hold at 1 percent, a hawkish inflation warning, higher front-end yields, and further sharp yen moves on July 31.  
> **Reported:** Reuters cited a market source identifying Japanese intervention on July 30 and reported U.S. rate checks and official support.  
> **Estimated:** settlement data implied intervention of as much as $58.97 billion.  
> **Unresolved at cutoff:** the official amount, precise operating dates, and whether every July 31 vertical move represented a new official tranche rather than positioning around intervention risk.

Even under that conservative classification, the analytical result is strong. The market priced a higher probability of another BOJ increase while refusing to treat the expected increase as sufficient for the currency. The bond market was no longer confident in an extended hold. The FX market was no longer confident that ending the hold would repair the yen.

This is dual-market rejection:

> the JGB market rejects the affordability or credibility of prolonged accommodation;  
> the currency market rejects the adequacy of the tightening that it expects Japan can deliver.

Neither verdict implies imminent crisis. Together they narrow the policy corridor. If the BOJ hikes, the fiscal and credit channels tighten. If it does not, the exchange-rate and inflation channels tighten. Intervention can interrupt the exchange-rate channel but does not uncouple the system.

## 5.9 Monitoring bands are reaction functions, not crash levels

Market analysis often turns observed levels into talismans: 160 or 165 on USD/JPY, 3 percent on the ten-year JGB, 4 percent on the thirty-year. Those numbers matter because investors, institutions, and officials coordinate around them. They do not possess a mechanical capacity to cause failure.

For this monograph, the useful signals are joint configurations rather than isolated thresholds:

| Indicator | Working monitoring band | What it may reveal |
|---|---:|---|
| USD/JPY | 164–165 | Whether intervention and expected BOJ tightening still deter renewed yen selling |
| 2-year JGB | Around 1.55% or higher | Further repricing of the near-term policy path |
| 10-year JGB | Around 2.90–3.00% | Collision among inflation, fiscal assumptions, and the budget’s rate framework |
| 30-year JGB | Around 4.00% or higher | Long-duration demand and fiscal-risk premium |

These are analytical bands drawn from the mid-2026 market, not universal boundaries. The thirty-year yield had already traded above 4 percent without disorder. USD/JPY had approached 164 and then been driven sharply lower. A breach is therefore not the event. Persistence and combination are what matter.

The more consequential signal would be simultaneous yen weakness and rising long yields—for example, USD/JPY sustaining levels above the intervention zone while the ten- and thirty-year sectors reprice upward. Under the textbook version of normalization, higher Japanese yields should support the yen at the margin. When yields and USD/JPY rise together, the market may be distinguishing between tighter domestic financing conditions and a monetary stance still judged insufficient relative to inflation and fiscal risk.

That configuration would place the BOJ in the most restrictive part of the corridor. Bond purchases could calm yields but risk further yen weakness. A faster rate increase could support the policy signal but transmit additional stress to borrowers and future budgets. Fiscal relief could protect households but reinforce issuance and credibility concerns. The importance of the monitoring bands lies in identifying that collision early, not predicting a single breaking point.

## 5.10 The domestic financial system transmits the constraint gradually

The BOJ’s capacity to raise rates depends partly on whether the financial system can absorb valuation and credit effects. The April 2026 *Financial System Report* found that banks remained broadly resilient and that yen interest-rate risk in banking books was low relative to capital in aggregate. It also showed adaptation already underway. Regional and shinkin banks had shortened the duration of yen-bond holdings, sold some low-yielding securities, and remained cautious about increasing JGB exposure. Major banks had added some medium- and long-term JGBs for carry income but maintained hedges and a cautious stance.[^21]

This evidence argues against a simple banking-crisis forecast. Japanese institutions are not passively holding the same duration exposure they carried under zero rates. They can reprice loans, increase deposit rates selectively, shorten securities portfolios, hedge, and realize gains or losses over time.

It also explains why the transition can tighten even without a crisis. Banks that avoid duration risk may be less willing to absorb long JGB supply at prevailing yields. Deposit competition raises funding costs. Weak borrowers refinance into higher loan rates. Institutions protect themselves individually while reducing the system’s former capacity to absorb government debt and private credit cheaply.

The same rate increase can improve bank lending margins and worsen credit quality. It can increase income on new securities and create losses on old low-coupon holdings. It can reward savers and raise mortgage payments. Aggregate resilience does not imply distributional neutrality.

Those transmission effects are slow enough to encourage further BOJ caution. Defaults, deposit migration, and loan repricing appear after the policy decision, not at the meeting table. By the time the full effect of a rate path is visible, several increases may already have been transmitted into contracts. This lag mirrors the fiscal refinancing mechanism in Chapter 4.

## 5.11 Counterarguments and limits

Five counterarguments qualify the chapter’s findings.

**First, the yen may be weak principally because U.S. rates are high.** The U.S. side is indispensable. At the cutoff, the Federal Reserve had kept its target range at 3.5–3.75 percent and three FOMC voters preferred an increase. A future decline in U.S. inflation and rates could strengthen the yen without aggressive BOJ action. That would widen Japan’s policy corridor. It would not remove the fiscal consequences of JGB yields already issued at higher coupons.

**Second, currency intervention may be more effective when coordinated.** Correct. Foreign rate checks, shared language, and joint operations can alter expectations more powerfully than unilateral action. The July episode showed that even the prospect of U.S. support moved the yen. Durable effectiveness still depends on whether coordination changes the expected policy path or merely increases the size and surprise of the official flow.

**Third, higher JGB yields may represent successful normalization rather than lost control.** They often do. A functioning sovereign market should price inflation, term, and supply risk. The thesis is not strengthened by every yield increase. It is strengthened when rising yields materially raise refinancing costs while the yen remains weak and the BOJ retains a large residual-buyer role.

**Fourth, the BOJ can stop long-yield disorder without abandoning rate hikes.** It can raise the overnight rate while purchasing longer bonds, separating the policy stance from market-function operations. That combination is technically available. Its credibility depends on the scale and duration of purchases. If used repeatedly, the distinction between liquidity support and yield suppression becomes harder for markets to maintain.

**Fifth, the practical compression zone may prove too low.** Japan’s firms, households, banks, and fiscal authorities may absorb policy rates above 1.5 percent with less damage than anticipated, especially if nominal income and productivity rise. That outcome would weaken a central element of the slow-compression thesis. The appropriate test is not whether a board member or economist identifies 2 percent as neutral, but whether the BOJ can approach that level while debt service, broad credit, real wages, bank balance sheets, and the yen remain stable together.

The event-study evidence also has limits. Intraday foreign-exchange moves cannot identify official orders with certainty. Headlines, option barriers, stop-losses, dealer hedging, and month-end flows can create sharp moves. Settlement estimates contain forecast error. Official quarterly data arrive with a lag. The July classification therefore separates confirmed market prices from reported intervention and estimated amounts.

These limitations weaken any claim that every vertical yen move was official. They do not weaken the broader conclusion that maintaining the currency required increasingly forceful intervention risk while the market simultaneously priced additional BOJ tightening.

## 5.12 Finding: monetary control survives, but independent effectiveness is narrowing

Japan has not lost the capacity to set interest rates, stabilize bond-market functioning, or intervene in foreign exchange. The BOJ can tighten further. It can slow or reverse the taper of JGB purchases. MOF can mobilize a large reserve portfolio. The monetary regime remains operational.

What is narrowing is the independent effectiveness of each instrument.

> Rate hikes support inflation credibility but accelerate fiscal and credit repricing.  
> Bond purchases contain yields but can weaken price discovery and the currency signal.  
> Intervention disrupts depreciation but decays when the policy differential remains.  
> Fiscal relief protects households but can reinforce doubts about the duration of monetary restraint.

The July 2026 episode condensed the system into two days. A large authority-driven yen move interrupted depreciation. The currency retraced much of that gain before the BOJ decision. The Bank held at 1 percent but issued its strongest warning yet about an inflation overshoot. Front-end yields rose as the market priced another hike. The yen still required the threat or presence of further official support.

That sequence does not establish that a September increase would fail. It establishes that the expectation of an increase was not enough. The bond market believed the BOJ might tighten sooner. The currency market believed the likely tightening path would remain insufficient relative to foreign rates and Japan’s fiscal-monetary configuration.

The monetary core of slow compression is therefore not paralysis. It is adverse instrument coupling. The BOJ can act, but action in one market increasingly produces a cost in another. Monetary sovereignty remains legally intact while practical policy space contracts.

Chapter 6 follows those costs into the domestic economy. The question is no longer only whether rates rise or the yen falls. It is who absorbs the adjustment: households paying more for imports, firms refinancing at higher rates, large exporters receiving currency gains, SMEs losing margin, banks repricing assets and deposits, or the state compensating the affected groups through another fiscal layer.
