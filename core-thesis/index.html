    Core Argument¶

    Japan's problem is not that it has no tools. It is that using one tool increasingly damages the conditions required for the others to work.

    The claim¶

    Japan is moving from policy-controlled stability toward market-driven constraint. The state remains financeable, the banking system remains functional, the Bank of Japan retains operational control, and the Ministry of Finance retains a large reserve portfolio. Those facts explain why sudden collapse is the wrong baseline.

    They do not establish unlimited policy freedom.

    The old regime depended on an unusual alignment: weak inflation, negligible interest rates, domestic financial surpluses, long debt maturities, BOJ absorption, strong external assets, corporate caution, and political tolerance for incremental adjustment. That alignment made an extraordinary public-debt stock manageable at low annual cost.

    Inflation and monetary normalization are now breaking the alignment before fiscal repair is complete. Higher marginal yields enter the budget through refinancing. A weak yen raises import costs and nominal revenue. Household relief moves those costs into borrowing or lost tax capacity. Rate increases can defend inflation credibility while repricing public debt, mortgages, SMEs, and bank portfolios. Each instrument remains usable. Fewer combinations remain mutually compatible.

    The false binary¶

    Rejected claim

    Imminent collapse

    A high gross-debt ratio does not assign a crisis date. Yen denomination, long maturities, BOJ capacity, domestic institutions, private wealth, and external assets make a conventional sovereign funding stop an inappropriate base case.

    Rejected claim

    No meaningful constraint

    The ability to issue yen does not make the distributional, inflationary, political, credit, or real-resource cost disappear. Financeability is not the same thing as freedom of action.

    Four findings¶

    1. Refinancing is the fiscal clock¶

    The debt stock does not reprice at once. Legacy bonds mature gradually and are replaced at current yields. This delay prevents an immediate shock, but it also embeds higher carrying costs across successive budgets. The operative constraint is the annual flow of debt service and the fiscal room remaining after it—not a single gross-debt threshold.

    2. Monetary control survives; separability does not¶

    The BOJ can raise rates, alter JGB purchases, and preserve market functioning. MOF can intervene in foreign exchange. What is narrowing is the independent effectiveness of each instrument:

    Rate hikes support credibility but accelerate fiscal and credit repricing.
    Bond purchases contain yields but weaken price discovery and the currency signal.
    Intervention disrupts depreciation but decays when the policy differential remains.
    Fiscal relief protects households but can reinforce doubt about the durability of restraint.

    3. Distribution is the adjustment mechanism¶

    Aggregate resilience is financed by unequal absorption. Households reduce discretionary consumption; large firms use overseas income, cash, and pricing power; SMEs absorb unrecovered costs through margins and borrowing; banks gain from asset repricing before weaker credits and old securities create delayed losses; the state compensates the most visible constituencies.

    This allocation prevents synchronized failure. It also weakens the private demand and investment required for durable stabilization.

    4. Demography removes the margin for deferral¶

    Demographic contraction is not the first spark and does not impose a sovereign-crisis date. It reduces the number of workers, taxpayers, customers, caregivers, borrowers, local institutions, and balance sheets available to absorb every other adjustment. It is the reduction in firebreaks.

    The base case¶

    The dominant path is managed compression:

    • gradual BOJ normalization that remains short of clearly restrictive territory;
    • a weak, volatile, and intervention-sensitive yen;
    • JGB auctions that continue to clear at a higher marginal cost;
    • recurring fiscal relief and supplementary budgets;
    • aggregate resilience alongside a widening vulnerable tail;
    • demographic adaptation sufficient to prevent breakdown but insufficient to restore broad fiscal room.

    This can persist for years. Long refinancing lags, large balance sheets, central-bank capacity, and political compensation disperse the cost. The regime changes when yields, the exchange rate, auctions, credit conditions, and portfolio demand begin to dictate policy sequencing faster than elected institutions would otherwise choose.

    What the thesis does not claim¶

    The monograph does not claim imminent insolvency, a predetermined USD/JPY path, a fixed BOJ ceiling, a scheduled banking crisis, or a single demographic cliff. The 1.25–1.5 percent policy-rate zone, the approximately 50-trillion-yen debt-service stress result, and the market levels discussed in the scenarios are monitoring tools and conditional estimates—not official targets or guaranteed outcomes.

    The thesis is falsifiable. It weakens if real wages, productivity, private consumption, primary balances, JGB demand, energy resilience, credit conditions, and demographic capacity improve together for several years without an equivalent liability being shifted into another box.
